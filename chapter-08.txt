“Mother, I see you looking out the window every morning. What are you looking at?” asked Fritz. “I look for clouds.” “Clouds?” “Yes, I’ve always loved clouds—white and fluffy, dark and looming, tinged with pink, edged with light. They are amazing.” “I never knew you liked clouds.” Maria cracked open the window. Cool morning air rushed in, carrying the smell of new, growing things and the song of birds—the high tweets of collared flycatchers, the knocking of woodpeckers, and the low hoots of the tufted crest hoopoe. Fritz heard coins jingling in her apron pocket. “I hear your butter money, Mother. You’ve been to the neighbors. I’ve never wondered until now—what do you do with the money you earn selling butter?” “I’m saving it for something special.” “What?” “Well, I want a gas oven. They don’t let off smoke. They don’t make the kitchen unbearably hot in the summer, and they cook things so much faster than our cast-iron stove.” She stared out the window again, as if daydreaming. Then she turned to Fritz, a big smile on her face. “We have a surprise for you today. I’ll go get everyone.” Within a few minutes, the family was gathered round Fritz in the parlor. His bed had been pushed over to the wall by the window since it wasn’t cold enough anymore for him to need the stove’s warmth. “As you all know, the doctor came yesterday,” began Jakob. “He said Fritz is healed enough to be moved around.” “He can walk now?” gasped Amalia. “No,” said Jakob, “it will be a little while until he tries that, Amalia. But he can be moved now. He no longer has to stay in bed all day.” “I see what he’s doing. Father is ready to have me milk the cow and weed the garden,” teased Fritz. Everyone laughed. “It will actually be a while before Fritz gains enough strength to sit up for very long, as he has been lying down for weeks,” said Maria. “But the doctor did suggest he spend as much time resting outdoors as he can this spring.” Gretchen clapped her hands. “Guess what! We are taking you outside today, Fritz!” The family slid a blanket under Fritz, gathered round him, grabbed the edges of the blanket, and carried Fritz outside, all while jubilantly singing. The world never looked so beautiful and full of life to Fritz after the long dreary winter of pain and confinement. Herr Engel appeared on top of the hill and gave a hearty wave. Fritz felt as light and free as the little cuckoo bird that just sailed past them. After laying Fritz down carefully by the garden, Maria and the girls went to gather the breakfast. “We are having a breakfast picnic for you, Fritz!” said Agatha. “I didn’t even know there was such a thing as a breakfast picnic.” And she twirled off. Elsie and Peter kept Fritz propped up in a sitting position. It wasn’t easy for him to stay upright, since his muscles were very weak from lying down all winter. After breakfast, little Flora picked a barely sprouted pea plant from the spring garden and toddled over to Fritz. “For my Fritz.” Fritz laughed, “Why, thank you.” Amalia ran to a fruit tree and picked a blossom for Fritz. Agatha, not to be outdone by her twin sister, picked seven blossoms and carefully placed them in Fritz’s hair, in a curve like a crown. “Now you are a king,” she announced. “I think you are adored,” Elsie whispered to Fritz. Fritz bit his lip to keep his eyes from tearing up. When Peter went off to help with chores, an uninvited sadness welled up in Fritz. His brother was so strong. It seemed incredible that Peter could just jump up and start walking. How is it fair that I can never do that again? But a scripture came into Fritz’s mind. He and Elsie had read it yesterday and had laughed at it: “Envy slayeth the silly one.” Fritz smiled. The scripture had been funny, but now he found it quite profound. “What are you smiling at?” Elsie asked him. “Oh, I’m just reminding myself not to be a ‘silly one’ and be slayed by envy.” Elsie nodded understandingly as she watched Peter walk away. Later that day, Fritz was propped up against the front of the house. He had nothing to do but watch everyone. Peter whistled when he worked outside. I never noticed that, mused Fritz. He had been especially fascinated to see Peter pick up a handful of dirt in the garden and let it sift through his hands as he smelled it. He really loves working with the land. Massive fluffy clouds rolled in, and Fritz asked Elsie to get him some of his paper and his fountain pen. Wanting to do something special for his mother, he tried to draw the clouds. He held his work up when he was done. “Oh dear,” he said aloud, grimacing at the amateur picture. Just then, their goat Nodo came click-clacking over the cobblestones to Fritz. The goat took a big bite out of the paper. “Yes, eat the whole thing,” said Fritz. The goat was happy to oblige. Guarding his next piece of paper from the hungry goat, Fritz wrote: To Mother: Heavenly white and fringed with light Clouds sail in the sea above. Made by God to bless the earth They are a witness of His love. Already the words of the Bible were sinking deep into Fritz’s soul. He was seeing God in all things. He folded the paper in quarters and asked Elsie to place it in the kitchen for Mother to find. Later that day, Fritz was propped up in his bed in the parlor when Peter and Father entered the room and sat at the table. “We need to talk about the farm,” Jakob said, getting right to the point. “Fritz, as you have probably realized, you won’t be able to help with planting or harvesting the fields. The farm will need to go to Peter.” “So Peter won’t work at the Hotel Zum?” asked Fritz. “No, he won’t.” “Good,” said Fritz. “Peter loves this land. This is the best news for you, isn’t it, Peter?” “Yes,” said Peter excitedly. “Oh, Father, I have so many ideas. We can enlarge the fields to the east, and there are new farming methods I have read about, and there are new crops we should try, and I have this idea of adding more beehives and selling the excess honey. I also . . .” He stopped and looked at Fritz in alarm. “Oh, Fritz, I’m so sorry. Are you sad about losing the farm to me?” “No, Peter, I’m very happy for you.” Fritz had never wanted the farm, but now that he couldn’t have it, he did feel a loss. At the same time, he knew with all his heart that Peter belonged on the farm. He had not seen Peter so excited about anything in a long time. However, Fritz’s heart sank with what he heard next. “Fritz, there’s nothing to keep you from making clocks with me,” his father announced. “It will be a sufficient living for you. You can even do it year-round once you master the skills. We’ll follow the doctor’s order and let you heal this spring, and then we will be ready to get started in late fall when the snow hits.” “Yes, Father.” Fritz forced a smile. Painful words swirled in his mind: disabled, excused from school attendance, sufficient living—none of the words he wanted describing his life. “You know,” said Jakob, studying his son’s face, “Karl Hofer got his start as an artist by painting clock faces.” “I didn’t know that,” said Fritz, feeling no excitement. “Your legs may keep you from farming, Fritz, but there are still things you can do in life. I think clockmaking will suit you well. And you never know, maybe you’ll end up following in the footsteps of Karl Hofer.” “Yes, Father. Thank you.” Jakob and Peter left, and Fritz could hear them down the hallway, Peter talking excitedly about plans for the farm before they disappeared outside. Fritz was alone on his bed. The ticking of the parlor clock seemed to echo more loudly than normal in the room. The setting sun threw beams of light on the Bible lying on the table, too far away to reach. Fritz longed for it. I can’t even get my Bible six feet away, thought Fritz. His muscles were fatigued, and he needed to lie down, but his legs were like dead weights, and his hip still hurt. He found he could hardly move his position. Pain wracked his body. He felt completely helpless. Elsie appeared in the room and saw his tears. She helped him lie down and then handed him a little wooden bowl of roasted hazelnuts she had brought for him. “Your favorite. They’re piping hot.” She grabbed the Bible and sat in the sunlight at the table. “I think we both need this tonight,” she said as she opened the worn pages. To Fritz, Elsie seemed like an angel. She read for half an hour, the words sinking into their souls like light shining on little seedlings. After a while, Elsie’s good eye was too fatigued to read anymore. She closed the book with a contented sigh and then knelt by Fritz’s bed and laid her head on his arm. “Things will get better, Fritz. I promise. Every day things will get easier and easier.”
