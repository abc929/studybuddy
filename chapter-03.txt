The twittering of birds roused Fritz from a fitful sleep. As he pushed his way out of the pine boughs of his shelter, he saw clumps of little purple wildflowers waving gently in the light breeze. He breathed in the fresh scent of dirt after rain and listened to a merry chorus of birds overhead. It felt as if the forest was mocking him by being so delightful and happy while also holding him prisoner. He looked around and had no idea which direction to go, so he implored God to help him find a way out of the forest. The trees were often thick and the forest dim. Fritz trotted along as quickly as possible, sometimes on a path he found, sometimes not on a path, feeling that God was guiding him somehow, even though the forest seemed to never end. He saw numerous squirrels and caught glimpses of two rabbits and a group of deer. Otherwise, he felt all alone in the world as he forged a few rivers and jogged along, the fallen pine needles and moss muting the sound of his footsteps. Several hours later, exhausted and weak from hunger, Fritz finally found his way out of the forest and trudged into the dazzling daylight of afternoon. Broad, gently rolling hills carpeted in green grass opened before him, fringed by forests. Scanning the vast scene before him, Fritz couldn’t see any signs of people—not even cattle grazing on the hillsides. He felt as small and lost as a blade of grass under the boundless sky. His eyes began to sting with tears, and his throat ached from holding back the sob that was trying to escape. Maybe there is a farm tucked somewhere in these hills. All I can do is go forward, Fritz thought. I am not going back into the forest. How many miles Fritz walked, he didn’t know. Descending a hill, walking across a meadow, ascending a long slope, descending back into a valley, crossing a gurgling brook, mounting another hill—on and on he went. As the sun was getting low in the sky, Fritz crested another hill, squinted his eyes, and shouted. “Hello! Hello! Hello!” A person was sitting on top of the next hill quite a ways off. It was a man. It seemed that the man did not hear him. Am I seeing things? thought Fritz. What is he doing just sitting there— “Hello!” Fritz shouted as he cupped his hands by his mouth. When it was apparent that the man still didn’t hear him, Fritz ran down the hill and plodded up the next as best as his weary body could go. Fritz appeared suddenly at the top of the hill and half-stumbled over to the man. “Can you help me, please?” he implored. The startled man looked at Fritz with wide eyes. It was then that Fritz noticed what the man was doing. Painting! “Are you—are you Karl Hofer?” said Fritz slowly. “Yes, I am, but what in the world are you doing here? You . . . are you all right?” “No, I got lost in the fog yesterday. I wandered in the forest and slept there. For almost two days, I’ve eaten nothing more than berries.” “Oh, you must be starving!” said the man as he reached for a basket and took out food. “I don’t have much food left, but here, you must take it.” Fritz sat down and gratefully ate the piece of bread and thin slice of cheese that the man offered, trying not to devour the food impolitely. He then gulped down a jar of apple cider the man had also given him. Fritz thought he could’ve eaten a loaf of bread as big as the basket, but he was grateful for at least a little food. “Wait!” said Fritz. “My teacher said that you were looking for a spot in Gutach Valley to paint. This isn’t Gutach Valley. I’d know that anywhere. It’s where I live.” “You’re right; it certainly isn’t. It’s a half day’s drive to Gutach Valley by coach. I was on my way there, but I stopped here instead when I spotted this beautiful area.” “Can you help me get home?” asked Fritz, desperate to get back to his family. “I, uh, I don’t know about that. You see, I’m waiting for the sunset so that I can paint it—it will begin any second now. The clouds all stretched out in the sky like strands of wispy cotton are going to make a most marvelous sunset. I have a friend picking me up here soon, once the sunset is done . . . but we have a schedule. I have to get back to the city . . . I . . .” The man frowned. “Look, do you see the road down there?” Fritz followed the man’s pointing finger. The road was no pathway that would dead-end in the forest. This was a well-traveled road. “If you go down to that road, someone is sure to come along and pick you up,” said the man. “I’ve seen at least a couple of people come by every hour. Who knows—maybe they’ll even be headed directly to Gutach Valley. I won’t be going there anymore.” Karl Hofer turned back to his paints and began mixing colors on his palette. He seemed to have forgotten all about Fritz as he studied the sky and adjusted his canvas. All Fritz could think of was getting home. “Thank you, Herr Hofer,” he said. “You were kind to feed me. I will head down to the road.” Fritz was not on the road long when someone did come along—going very fast! It was a boy around his age on a sleek, black horse. Fritz waved and hollered from the side of the road, but it seemed the boy did not see or hear him. In a moment the boy was gone around the next bend, and only puffs of dust remained. Fritz, severely disappointed, kept walking. Around two more curves in the road, Fritz saw a sight that made him suck in his breath. There in the middle of the road was the boy that had ridden past him. He lay flat on the ground, obviously injured, and his horse was standing near the edge of the road, nibbling grass. As he ran to the boy, Fritz noticed that dusk had arrived and long shadows were falling across the road. “Are you OK?” Fritz inquired, kneeling by the boy. “No,” moaned the boy. “A stag darted across the road, and he reared up. I fell off, and my shoulder—I think it’s broken. And maybe my wrist. Oh, it hurts to move even one bit!” The boy’s face was white and twisted with pain. Fritz quickly realized there was no way he could get back on his horse, let alone hold on once he did. “Please,” started the boy. “Take my horse. Just let him lead. He knows the way home. Get my family. They will bring their coach.” “Oh, I—I’m sorry, I’ve never ridden a horse. I couldn’t possibly do that. And what if the horse doesn’t go home? What if he wanders into the forest?” Fritz shuddered at the thought of being lost in the forest again—maybe this time in the dark on a horse that he didn’t even know how to stop. “It’s not that far by horseback. Oh, please hurry,” said the boy, looking at Fritz with imploring eyes. “It’s going to be dark. I can’t bear this pain, and I don’t want to be out here all alone in the dark. Please, I’m begging you.” “Maybe we should wait for someone to come along?” offered Fritz. “You should scoot to the side of the road.” “It’s getting dark; another traveler is unlikely,” gasped the boy as he scooted over to the side of the road. “Please help me! My home is less than a thirty-minute ride from here.” “I will try,” Fritz agreed. With a little coaching from the boy, Fritz awkwardly climbed on the horse and got it going. He did not even direct the horse with the reins but let him go. The horse broke into a slow gallop, and Fritz almost screamed. After a minute, though, Fritz found it thrilling to be riding the horse with its steady gait, and although he didn’t feel quite safe, he loved the feel of the wind in his hair. Sure enough, the horse did arrive at a house within a few minutes. Fritz went to the door of the beautiful dwelling and knocked loudly. The windows glowed with light against the now very dim sky, and faint voices and the clatter of dishes being washed seeped outside from the cracks around the door frame. How comforting to see a home full of light and people! thought Fritz. The door swung open, and Fritz quickly told the family about their son, and then everything was a blur to Fritz’s exhausted mind. The family was in a panic and soon left in their coach, completely forgetting about him. Not feeling as if he could keep his eyes open a moment longer, and shivering in the cool night breeze, Fritz wandered over to a garden shed, walked in, and shut the door to the breeze. He found a canvas tarp to roll up in and fell asleep before he could even review in his mind the astonishing events he had just experienced. When Fritz stepped out of the shed in the morning, a girl across the neatly trimmed lawn spotted him and started shouting. “He’s still here! It’s him! Mother! Mother!” A slim woman in a fashionably tailored dress came running out of the spacious home. “Oh, you dear boy! I’m so very sorry we forgot all about you. Come,” she gestured toward her home. “Roman—that’s my son who was injured— he told us all about how you’d never ridden a horse and how you helped him. You must come in and tell us what you were doing walking on that road at dusk and what we can do to repay you.” Within fifteen minutes, Fritz had told his story to the Winkler family while managing to eat six slices of hot bacon, two eggs fried in butter, and five little pancakes smeared with heavenly strawberry preserves. “Unbelievable,” said Herr Winkler. “My boy, you were so brave to get up on that horse.” “How is Roman?” inquired Fritz. “I fetched the doctor last night,” began Herr Winkler. “Roman does indeed have a broken shoulder, but his wrist is only sprained. The doctor said he should heal, although he’ll have to stay in bed for some time. He was in pain all night, but he is finally sleeping peacefully.” Frau Winkler gasped. “Your mother! She must be beside herself with worry about you, Fritz!” “Yes, we need to get the boy back home,” agreed Herr Winkler. He scratched his chin. “Fritz, what do you think of this plan? I can have one of my workers ride to your home on horseback and tell your family you are safe with us. With your parents’ permission, you could stay here for a week or so, let us show you our gratitude, and you could keep Roman company. Then we can take you by coach back home.” Fritz looked around as he sipped a cup of steaming chocolate. The elegant chairs, the papered walls, the porcelain teapot inlaid with gold—his surroundings were nothing like his humble home. This was obviously a very prominent family. Fritz was anxious to get home, but his interest in the wealthy lifestyle of the Winklers was strong. He would love to see what it was like to stay in a home such as this for a while. “I like your plan, Herr Winkler. It is very generous. Thank you.” “I like the plan, too!” said Roman’s little sister, clapping her hands. Her broad smile revealed two pleasant dimples in her cheeks. “Wonderful! I will draw up a hot bath and get you some fresh clothes,” said Roman’s mother. “And I will get my worker on his way to your home immediately,” said Herr Winkler. “Your parents will soon know that you are safe.”
