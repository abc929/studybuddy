As the Winklers’ carriage pulled up to the Vogel farmhouse, a few sprinkles of rain dropped from the sky. Fritz’s heart sank as he saw his family working frantically in the field by their home. He had wondered what his homecoming would be like, but he hadn’t expected this. The hay—Fritz could see that his family had cut it while he was gone. Now, after a few days in the sun, it was dry, but by the look of the angry sky, the hay would not be dry much longer. The family couldn’t afford for the hay to be ruined. Their cow was lost and their goat could not make it through the winter without the hay, and they usually had their neighbor take a wagonload or two of their extra hay into town to sell for them, which brought in much-needed money. Their neighbor, an older man named Herr Engel, was running spryly down the hill with his hay rake to help. Fritz and the Winkler family descended from the carriage not far from the field. Peter was working as if he were doing the work of two men, and maybe that was because Father was not there. Where is he? wondered Fritz. Something is not quite right. Fritz’s mother and Elsie were working intensely, their hair held back with handkerchiefs and their dirty aprons flapping in the wind. Fritz glanced at Mrs. Winkler in her fine clothing, with her well-set hair and her delicate jewelry. He could not for the life of him picture her ever working in the field like his mother and sister. He did, however, expect Herr Winkler and Roman to jump in and help save the hay. Black Forest farmers, strangers or friends, wouldn’t hesitate to help in a situation like this. Fritz rolled up his sleeves. “The hay must be saved! There is no time to lose!” declared Fritz. “Of course! You must go right away,” agreed Herr Winkler. “I’ll have Roman set your things under the eaves of your home, in case the rain picks up. We wanted to meet your parents, but I’m anxious to get back on the road before it turns muddy. All the best to you, Fritz. We will be in touch.” Surprised, but grateful for all the kindness the Winklers had shown him, Fritz nodded and ran to the field. There was no time for words. Peter quickly tossed Fritz his hay rake and then ran with the fully loaded cart to dump it in the safety of the hayloft. Accessed from the back of the house, which was built into the hillside, the hayloft was the top level of their home, and Peter could run into the hayloft directly from the hill. Just fifteen minutes later, rain swept over the field. It quickly drenched the group, and Fritz noticed that it carried the chill of late fall with it. The hay, however, all except one stack, had been saved. The Vogel family and Herr Engel gathered in the parlor, dripping wet. After giving Fritz a joyful hug and a misty-eyed smile, Frau Vogel went into the kitchen to start the evening meal. Fritz’s little sisters sat at the table. Amalia and Agatha, in their usual dramatic way, flew to Fritz and hugged him round the middle, crying, “Fritz, oh dear Fritz! You are safe! You are home!” Gretchen, however, did not get up from the table. She held Flora in her lap and glared at Fritz. “Not forgiven yet, am I, little Gretchen?” asked Fritz with a sparkle in his eye. Gretchen sat up straight. “I lost a tooth, just so you know. You didn’t know because you weren’t here. And I got a new cat. Father gave her to me, and I named her Muschi. And do you know why we got a new cat? Because our cat died while you were gone, and you didn’t even know that because you weren’t here. And Peter had to bury the cat, and Nelly is also lost forever.” “Nelly never came back?” Fritz gasped, the twinkle going out of his eye. “No. Father says we’ll never find her now,” said Gretchen. “And poor Flora has not had milk or butter all this week—none of us have, and Mother has not been able to sell extra butter to the neighbors anymore.” “Now, Gretchen,” said Elsie gently. “The cow being lost is not Fritz’s fault at all. Fritz actually went through quite the ordeal because he tried to bring Nelly home. Fritz is very brave, and we are grateful to him. And besides, we have been able to have goat milk.” “Humph, he didn’t have to stay away,” muttered Gretchen. “None of us like goat milk, especially Flora. We like goat cheese, but now we can’t make cheese because we are drinking all the goat milk.” “Gretchen, dear, again, the missing cow is not Fritz’s fault. We are so happy to have Fritz home safely, aren’t we?” Elsie said cheerfully. “Well, he could have come home sooner. Father is sick, Fritz, and Peter has had to do all your work and Father’s work. Why didn’t you come home sooner?” “I—I didn’t know,” he turned to Peter in alarm. “How is Father? What happened?” “He got a cough, Fritz, and then yesterday afternoon he came down with a fever. He still has a fever today, but it’s only mild. Mother has him tucked in bed, and she thinks he will be OK. We are very glad you are home, Fritz,” said Peter kindly, making no mention of the extra work he had picked up to cover for Fritz’s absence. “And just in time. You helped us save the hay!” Peter then turned to their elderly neighbor, who was almost like a member of the family. “Herr Engel, we thank you for your help. What a blessing that you came when you did. We are deeply grateful for the friendship and support of such a fine neighbor.” Ever noble, ever good Peter, thought Fritz, but how can you always be so calm and good? Where are your emotions? Are you keeping everything bottled up inside? The next morning, the sun hid behind a slate-gray sky. Fall was trying very hard to pull winter into the land. During the Vogel children’s long walk to school, the breeze tugged the last of the brown and orange leaves from the trees and sent them twirling in the air. Little puffs of breath, made visible by the cold, surrounded the group. Amalia and Agatha pretended that the leaf-covered pathway was a royal rug on which they were walking to meet a queen. Gretchen kept her eyes glued to the trees, in search of any migratory birds that might still be in the area. Elsie quizzed Fritz endlessly on his stay with the Winkler family, engrossed in every detail. Peter interjected a question here and there and seemed particularly interested in Roman’s wish to have a brother. Fritz smiled as they approached the little schoolhouse. He loved everything about school—the smell of the chalk, the map on the wall, the small cabinet of books, and the youthful excitement of their young teacher. To get somewhere in life, Fritz knew he needed to do well in school. His teacher demanded strict obedience, but today something happened that deeply tested his adherence to obedience. It occurred near the end of the school day. During a lecture on botany, Fritz happened to glance out the window and thought he saw something behind a bush. He leaned forward slightly and peered at the bush carefully. The bush moved, and Fritz saw the outline of . . . a person! Fritz gasped quietly, but loudly enough that Peter bumped Fritz’s knee, warning him to pay attention to the teacher. Fritz, however, no longer heard a word that his teacher was saying. A head popped out of the bush. The brown braids on the little girl seemed familiar. Then a red-headed girl appeared and started talking to the girl. There was no doubt—they were the girls he’d seen at the tree with the door. “Unbelievable!” cried Fritz. The teacher stopped talking and looked at Fritz with a quizzical expression. The room seemed to be holding its breath, waiting to see what would happen next. The teacher frowned. “Well, I’m glad this information is fascinating to you, Fritz, but you know better than to make any disruptions to my class. That is quite uncalled for.” Fritz bowed his head slightly to show his understanding, and the teacher continued. It’s them. Oh, I have to find out who they are. Fritz was thinking seriously of leaving the classroom—just flying out the door—despite the severe consequences that he knew would follow. He might never see the girls again. They were leaving! Totally unaware of the teacher’s lecture, Fritz gasped very loudly. The girls disappeared into the trees, and Fritz called out, “Oh, my! Oh, my!” The teacher stopped abruptly. Twenty-eight students sat hushed—their wide eyes trained on Fritz. “Really, Fritz!” said the teacher, sounding both baffled and upset. “I had no idea you found botany so intriguing, but you really must keep your noises to yourself!” The color was rising in the teacher’s face, showing that the teacher was getting very unhappy, so Fritz made the terribly hard decision to stay in his seat. When class ended, Fritz asked Peter if he could walk the girls home since he needed to talk to Elsie. He told her all about Roman’s plan to change places next summer, and the girls he had seen and the door in the tree, and how Roman had asked him to write the story he had imagined about the girls, and how he’d left before it was finished. “You didn’t finish the story?” Elsie exclaimed. “No, it kept growing. Roman said to take it home and finish it. I’ll read it to him later when we meet in the summer to change places. He gave me a fountain pen, ink, and a huge box of paper.” Elsie was impressed. They had never had paper or a pen at their house before. “Is your story really going to be that long?” “No, he gave me extra paper so I could practice drawing, you know, to help me one day become an artist.” Fritz hesitated for a moment, but he had made up his mind, so he plowed forward. “He also gave me something else—a bag of money. I refused it, but he insisted. He was so grateful for my courage in getting up on that horse and helping him. He said I can use the money to help me get some art supplies and maybe even some art lessons in town. But . . . I’ve decided to do something else with the money.” He stopped and looked at his twin sister. “We need to stop by Farmer Becker’s on the way home and buy our family another cow.”
