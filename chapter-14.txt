The morning was breezy. Clouds scuttled across the sky as if in a hurry, and the trees around the little schoolhouse swayed back and forth. Fritz enjoyed the cool morning air as he sat outside the schoolhouse. Excited that the day of the poetry competition was finally here, he had arrived at school early to go through his stack of poems. Now his ten best were on top, held down by a big rock. Fritz leaned back against the smooth wood of the schoolhouse and closed his eyes, letting the cool air wash over his face. “What do we have here?” Günter’s gravelly voice startled Fritz a minute later. Fritz looked up to find Günter holding his stack of poems. “Günter, those are my poems. Please give them back.” Günter let one of the pages fly loose in the wind. “Oh, no. So sorry, Fritz!” mocked Günter. As he ran after the poem, he let the other pages loose. “Oh, no! What an accident!” he cried insincerely. The pages scattered in all directions, whirling up in the wind. Fritz struggled to his feet, knowing he could never gather his poems. To make things worse, the wind picked up. Most of the pages were out of sight now. Some had flown toward the back of the schoolhouse. “I’ll get them!” cried Günter, disappearing behind the schoolhouse. He returned a minute later with his satchel still on his shoulder, but no papers in his hands. “I’m afraid they are all gone,” said Günter. “All of them have been whirled away. If you tell the teacher, I’ll explain that I was just looking at all of your papers, so impressed with your poems, when the wind picked up and blew them away. I’ll tell him how very sorry I am for this complete accident that no one else was here to see.” Fritz’s chest and throat ached from holding back tears. He wasn’t easily prone to anger anymore; the words of Christ were too much a part of his heart, but he was filled with sorrow and deep disappointment. He had poured his heart into those poems. “Why?” he asked Günter quietly. “Why do you hurt us?” Günter shrugged. “I don’t know what you mean, Fritz. It was an accident.” Fritz closed his eyes for a few moments. Then he opened them to look at Günter. “I’m sorry, Günter.” “You’re sorry? For what? For almost crying?” “I’m sorry for whatever has hurt you and made you want to hurt others in return.” Günter stared at Fritz, at first with questioning, surprised eyes, but then with eyes that narrowed and hardened. “I forgive you,” said Fritz. “I don’t want your forgiveness!” “I give it to you anyway.” The words were spoken kindly and quietly. Günter had no retort. He turned and walked into the classroom. Fritz pulled a pencil and a blank sheet of paper from his satchel. The tears flowed freely now and dripped on the page as he wrote. I Cannot Hate By Fritz Vogel There is one who has brought me pain, Who thoughtlessly has hurt my heart, And the dreams that I have held so close He has sought to tear apart. But he, a beloved child of God, I cannot hate nor despise, For though his actions bring me pain, He is precious in God’s eyes. A face and hands that God hath formed, A beating heart that God holds near, I cannot hate the man because God considers him so dear. When it was time to turn in the poems, most of the students came up and set their entries on the teacher’s desk. Some brought just one poem, but most brought several. Günter pulled a single, somewhat crumpled sheet out of his satchel and set it on the teacher’s desk. “I’ll take your poems up, Fritz,” said Elsie, stopping at his desk. “I want to bring mine up myself,” Fritz replied. Everyone else was back in their seats by the time Fritz reached the desk. There was something about Fritz’s unusually somber mood that made the room very quiet. Fritz laid his one sheet of paper on the top of the stack of poems. “Fritz,” said Elsie, loud enough for everyone to hear. “Where are the rest?” “They blew away,” he said simply as he made his way back to his desk. Fritz caught Günter looking at him. The haughtiness was gone, and Fritz saw a haunting regret in Günter’s eyes. It’s true, Fritz said to himself, I cannot hate him. But he couldn’t stop thinking about his precious poems either, still whirling away, probably being torn up on tree limbs. He felt sick. After a few minutes, Fritz raised his hand and asked if he could be excused to go home. The teacher studied Fritz’s downcast face and nodded. A little into the woods, Fritz stopped. The wind had died down to a murmuring breeze, and the woods were cool and peaceful. Fritz bowed his head and cried. So much of the difficulty of the past few months mingled with Fritz’s sorrow over his lost poems. Pain seemed to swirl around him. After a good cry, he took a deep breath, squared his shoulders, and said aloud, “Now it is time to move on and be of good cheer.” He prayed until peace filled him and hope buoyed up his heart again. I wonder if I can find any of my poems, he thought. He turned his horse off the path and trotted through the forest. After two hours of fruitless searching, he admitted to himself that none of the poems would be found. He was about to head back home when he came into a clearing. He suddenly sat erect, his eyes widening. “I’ve found it!” Fritz blurted aloud. “That is the tree!” It was just as beautiful as he remembered it. Its thick, twisting branches rocked gently in the wind. Fritz dismounted right next to the little door in the tree. Besides the scratching of branches in the breeze and the gentle rushing of leaves, it was silent. Slowly, he opened the little door. Nothing. Fritz thought for a moment. He had longed for this moment, but now he didn’t know what to do. Finding the tree hadn’t gotten him any closer to solving the mystery of the girls. He couldn’t wait there, hoping they showed up; the sun was sinking lower in the sky. He mounted Chocolate and turned toward home.
