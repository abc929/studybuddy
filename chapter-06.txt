The next day was Saturday, and Fritz woke up to a snow-covered world. A blanket of white encased the land in a quiet peace. Winter, it seemed, had finally made its full entrance. Fritz rubbed his cold hands but felt a warmth in his heart as he watched their new cow munching hay in the stables, which were a part of the house, as they were in most Black Forest farmhouses. He had milked the cow that morning and had given Gretchen two cups of warm, foamy milk. “One for you and one for Flora,” he’d said. Gretchen’s grudge seemed to melt a little. Peter was at Herr Engel’s home, just over the hill, helping him fix a cart, and Fritz had offered to do Peter’s chores, hoping it would help make up for the extra work Peter had had to do last week. Fritz began to whistle as he cleaned the stables. Father’s fever had broken earlier that morning. The world seemed good. His mind was roaming in another place, at another time, imagining the next part of his story. With no lamp available at night, he would have to find time to squeeze in some writing before the sun set. “Help! Fritz, help!” Gretchen came flying into the stables, her yellow braids bouncing wildly and the top of her head covered in big flakes of snow. “It’s Muschi and Elsie! Help!” Fritz threw down the shovel, alarmed at the panic in Gretchen’s voice. “Muschi? Oh, the new cat. What’s wrong?” “It’s Elsie! She needs help. Please come right now!” Fritz ran after Gretchen as she darted ahead of him to the river below their home. Swirling snow was falling in thick flakes now. At the same time that Fritz heard crying, Gretchen pointed up into the big tree at the river’s edge. “There was a badger,” started Gretchen, out of breath. “He scared Muschi out of her fur, and she ran up the tree. She couldn’t get down, so brave Elsie climbed up to get her. It wasn’t snowing until Elsie got up there.” “Fritz! I’m stuck!” called down Elsie through her tears. “My skirt is all tangled in a branch. I can’t get loose. My hands are so cold. Fritz, help me! I’m so scared. Oh, help!” She was absolutely frantic. Fritz could not believe how high up Elsie had gone. He began climbing. Thick snow was falling, making the branches slippery, so he had to go slowly. “You should have never gone up that high!” called Fritz, truly angry at his sister. “That was a foolish thing to do.” Fritz immediately regretted his words as fresh sobs came from Elsie. “I know. I’m sorry. It’s so cold, and I was worried about Muschi. Oh, Fritz! Be careful.” His hands were freezing now too, and the snow made it hard to see, but he kept pushing upward. When he reached Elsie, she let out a sob of relief. “Oh, Fritz. It’s my skirt right here. Do you see? Can you get it loose?” Fritz had to hold on to the tree with only one hand. His heart was beating fast. He knew this was dangerous, but what else could he do? He tugged on Elsie’s skirt. It didn’t come free. O, God help me! he prayed. He tugged harder and heard a rip. Elsie’s skirt was free, but she lost her balance. There was a scream. Fritz felt a heavy weight against him as Elsie fell. His grip on the tree was gone. Pain and thirst. “Water,” he whispered with great effort. A cup was almost instantly pressed to his lips, and a warm hand lifted his head slightly so he could drink. His eyes briefly focused on the face in front of him—his mother’s tear-streaked face— before he drifted off again. He was being roused. How much time had passed? Why can’t I move? A gentle hand was again lifting his head, and warm broth filled his mouth. He was aware of a cool, small hand pressed against his own hand and a soft little cheek to his ear and a familiar voice saying softly, “Oh, Fritz.” He moaned. His body, not able to handle the pain, offered him the sweet relief of unconscious￾ness again. Leave me alone. I cannot wake up. I cannot move, he thought as a hand lifted his head and a cup was again pressed to his lips. It was dark. The next time he was aroused, it was light. Time passed. Oh, the pain! He was aware of several little hands on his arms. A little cheek was again softly against his ear. Another “Oh, Fritz” was breathed against his hair. How sweet it felt. Gretchen. Time passed. Is that Father’s voice offering a prayer? “Father in Heaven, we need Thee. Give Peter speed. Bring him safely home.” Peter? Where is Peter? Where am I? Why can’t I move? But Fritz’s mind would not let him remember. Sunlight. It felt so warm and comforting, streaming on his face. Fritz’s eyes opened, but the sunlight hurt his eyes, and he shut them. “Mother?” “Fritz, I’m here.” “And I’m here,” piped a little voice. Gretchen. “And we are here,” added two little voices. Amalia and Agatha. A flitter of a smile crossed Fritz’s face. Fritz could open his eyes a little now. He caught a glimpse of the glazed, green tiles of the parlor stove. He felt a firm bed beneath him. “All of you, get water for the animals, please,” said Maria to her girls. “Gretchen, please tell your father that Fritz is awake.” “Why am I in a bed in the parlor?” asked Fritz. “I hurt so much.” “We wanted to keep you warm by the stove.” The tree! It all came back in a rush and made him feel as if he couldn’t breathe. His mother laid a cool, calming hand on Fritz’s forehead. Herr Vogel swept into the room and knelt down next to Fritz’s bed. “Son! Fritz. The doctor is coming, we pray. It’s been three long days, but the terrible storm has finally ended.” “What happened? I don’t remember. I was falling. But I don’t remember. Did I hit the ground?” “Yes,” said his father somberly. “Elsie! Did she fall too?” Fritz felt sick. Oh, dearest God, please, no. Not Elsie. Let Elsie be safe. “No, she did not fall all the way to the ground. She was able to grab a branch as she fell.” Tears streamed down Fritz’s face. “Is she OK?” “She—” Mother could not finish. “Fritz, she is hurt, but she is going to be OK. Don’t worry. Herr Engel has been here the past few days since the accident, and he is with her now.” “Father, I am not OK.” Fritz knew he wasn’t. “I’m in so much pain. What happened to me?” “You can move your arms, right, Fritz?” his father asked. They felt heavy, but Fritz could move his arms. He then felt bandages over his head and stinging on his face. He touched a bandage. “Good. It doesn’t appear that your arms are broken. Your face is covered in cuts and scrapes from the branches,” said Father. “Two of the cuts are very deep. The doctor will stitch them up. You have scrapes and bruises all over your body, but . . . what we are most worried about are your feet.” “What do you mean?” “Fritz, your feet are badly hurt,” his father said solemnly. “You mean they might be broken?” His father was silent for a few moments before speaking. “They are definitely broken. In fact . . .” “In fact, what, Father?” “Let’s wait until the doctor arrives,” said Maria gently. Fritz tried to move his legs. Not only could he not move them, but more pain shot through his body. “Mother, Father, what has happened to my legs?” Fritz cried in alarm. “I can’t move my legs. I can’t sit up. Please tell me what happened to my legs.” “Fritz, calm down and drink some broth and eat a little bread,” said Jakob. “Rest a little, and then we will tell you what we know.” Fritz ate some bread dipped in broth as his mother held up his head. Eating exhausted him, and he quickly fell into a deep slumber for a few hours. When he awoke, his mother and father were still there. “Tell me, Father. What happened to my legs?” Fritz said weakly. Jakob took a deep breath. “Your feet are badly swollen, and they are definitely broken. The doctor will tell us more.” Tears spilled out of Fritz’s eyes. “Father, you said the storm has been going for three days, and the doctor is coming?” Father sighed. “We hope so. The snowstorm turned into a raging blizzard as soon as we got you home. I could not go get the doctor in that blizzard, especially since I had just gotten over my fever and was quite weak. Peter begged to go himself. As much as we desperately needed a doctor, going out on foot in that blizzard was much too dangerous; we couldn’t let him go.” “Where is Peter, then?” Fritz interrupted. Fritz saw his mother’s eyes grow misty. “He went to get the doctor anyway,” she said. “He took your father’s snowshoes and left a note. We haven’t heard a word about him for three days.” Just then, there was a shout coming from the hallway. It was Peter’s voice! “I’m here! And I have brought Dr. Walter!”
