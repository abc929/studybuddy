As introductions were made, Fritz could hardly stand keeping the news in. After the Köhlers and Vogels and Beckers and Winklers had all met each other, Fritz gathered everyone around. “I just found a letter from Herr Engel. It was in his Bible. The letter says—well, here, I’ll read it.” Fritz held the letter in front of him as he read aloud. “Fritz, I know my time is short and I look forward to the day when I will meet my Savior. I have no living relatives, and I am leaving my farm and all that I own to you. You will find the deed to my home in a chest under my bed. You were such a joy to me in my life. May God be with you, Fritz, until we meet again. With love, Herr Engel.” Elsie brought her hands to her mouth in surprise. “Oh, Fritz! This is unbelievable.” “Where is Herr Engel’s farm?” asked Lisette. “Well, why don’t I show you! Let’s all go there right now,” cried Fritz. “Let’s have a picnic there,” suggested Maria. Everyone agreed to the idea, and Maria, Herr Becker, Herr Winkler, Lisette, and Elsie went inside to pack the food. The group was soon climbing up the hill, which was golden and gleaming in the fall sun. Alongside the group, Fritz rode Chocolate with Gretchen sitting behind him. When they reached the crest of the hill and Herr Engel’s farm came into view, Fritz was overcome with emotion. He loved that farm, and now it was his. It was almost too much to take in. “Oh!” cried Garit. “This is the most beautiful little area. Look at it, Lisette—the rolling hills, the stream running across the fields, the river tumbling over the big boulders, the woods that line the fields!” “It is beautiful, Garit,” Lisette cried. “Oh, Fritz! What a wonderful day! How much land comes with the farm?” Fritz pointed to the river far across the fields. “Herr Engel’s land goes from the river to the top of this hill to partway through the woods. It’s a lot of beautiful land.” “It’s your land, now,” said Peter cheerfully as he walked by Chocolate. “It’s much more land than we have at our farm. Oh, Fritz, I am so happy for you. This is just unbelievable!” Soon, the food was spread out on blankets and everyone was eating. Fritz knew that feeding this crowd took up more food than they had to spare from their winter store. He wondered how much money he would receive from publishing his books and how quickly he could get it. Elsie, Fritz, Peter, Roman, Lisette, and Garit all sat together. “Fritz,” started Elsie, “what are you going to do with this farm and this land?” Fritz looked around. “I can’t farm it, of course, but I don’t think I’ll need to. It looks like I will be able to support myself through writing and by being a minister. I couldn’t think of anything I’d rather do. I’d love to live in this house eventually, but I don’t really need the land.” A smile spread across Garit’s face. “Would you consider selling it?” he asked slowly. Fritz put a hand to his chin, thinking. “Sell it?” “To me,” Garit added. “Oh, yes!” Lisette cried. “This would be the perfect spot to build a little farmhouse.” “Sell it?” Fritz said again. “Why, that is a wonderful idea! I don’t need much of the land, and there is plenty of room to build another farmhouse.” “It is a marvelous idea!” exclaimed Peter, giving Garit a hearty pat on the back. “I could see our farmhouse right around the corner by those woods on the hillside over there,” said Garit, pointing. “You wouldn’t even see our farmhouse from your windows or your yard. Also, I wonder if we could lodge at Herr Engel’s home this summer and rent it from you until we can build our own home. We have money to pay you right now.” “This is absolutely perfect!” declared Fritz. He couldn’t wait to tell his father the news. Garit could pay right away, and there would now be plenty of money for the Vogel family to make repairs and to buy food for the winter and new farm equipment and even more animals. Fritz spotted his father talking to Günter. Günter had a stick in his hand that he had been whittling, and the two were chattering away. Jakob was laughing, and Günter was smiling. Hmm . . . Fritz thought. I wonder what they’re talking about. Fritz then looked around. He had learned over the past two years to become more observant of the things and people around him. Elsie and Roman were talking, and they looked as happy as could be. He noticed a blush on Elsie’s cheeks. Peter and Garit were talking, and Fritz realized that they, as neighboring farmers, would probably become great friends. Lisette had wandered over to the adults and was talking to Maria. Fritz knew that his mother would give a mother’s love to Lisette and Heidi and watch over them if they came to live nearby. He then saw that Gretchen had found a new friend in Heidi as the two of them were floating sticks down the stream. Fritz then looked past the group at the five graves of the Engel family under the large oak tree a little way beyond the house. He knew that Herr Engel would be happy that Fritz would live in his home one day and that the Köhlers would make their home on his land as well. He would take good care of those graves. After a few minutes, Fritz made his way slowly over to his father. Günter had gone back to sit with the Becker family. Fritz told his father his great news about selling some of the land to Garit. Then his father had some news himself. “Fritz, I know that you will not be helping me with clock making.” “I’m sorry, Father,” inserted Fritz. “Don’t be sorry, Fritz. It was not your calling. You’re a brilliant writer, and you will be an incredible minister. I’m so very happy for you. But how would you feel about me training Günter in clockmaking so he can work with me? He loves working with his hands, and he is incredibly interested in making clocks.” “Oh, Father,” laughed Fritz, “God has aligned everything so perfectly. It’s marvelous!” That night Fritz sat by his lantern in the parlor with the book about his life, which would be published next year. He finally penned the last words: “The End.” He put the stack of papers in order and stared at the title page: The True Story of Fritz Vogel by Fritz Vogel. Then, leaning back in his chair, he stared out at the canopy of twinkling stars, thinking of how he once had wanted his name to be famous. He sighed as he remembered bumping into the farmer’s cow and being offended that the farmer had called him the clockmaker’s son, as if the farmer didn’t realize how important Fritz was. “I am not the same person,” Fritz stated aloud. God has shown me a more beautiful story than the one I wanted to create for my life. Thoughts of his story flooded Fritz’s mind: a little girl’s voice whispering in his ear as he lay injured; Peter groaning and sweating as he tried to carry him to school; finding out that his mother had bought his horse; the cheerful laugh of his father; the steadfast love of his twin sister; the early morning sunlight as he read from the Bible; the wind in his hair as he rode Chocolate over the hills; the beauty of the forest that came to life as he wrote poems about it; Günter’s apology; and Herr Engel’s letter. Memories tumbled one over another, and Fritz’s heart felt as if it would burst with joy for the beauties he had found in his life. He picked up his fountain pen.
