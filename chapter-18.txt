Fritz propped up Herr Engel’s wooden disc on the windowsill of his father’s workshop and smiled at the words “Be of Good Cheer.” “Father, what can I help you with today?” Jakob looked up and saw the wooden disc. “You’ve put that up there every day for the past two weeks, Fritz. It seems you are needing those words. Are you doing OK? Maybe it’s time that you tell your teacher about the poem. Or, are you sad about missing the last part of school?” Fritz sighed. “I think Herr Engel knew I would need those words: ‘be of good cheer.’ They always help me. Yes, I do miss school, but I would be unhappy there knowing I could be helping you at home. I’ve come to realize that it’s OK to feel sad and disappointed, but it’s not good to dwell on those feelings. ‘Be of good cheer’—those words just breathe joy into me. After all, things are hard, but there is still so much to be cheerful about.” “Oh, like what?” said Jakob, handing Fritz some paper and paint. “Oh, like Frau Becker dropping off a bag of wheat for us today, and Flora dancing out there by the garden, and the golden grass waving in the breeze.” Jakob laughed and shook his head. “You could be happy in the middle of a tornado, I believe, Fritz.” “Well, I don’t know about that,” replied Fritz, and they both laughed. “Here I was,” said Jakob, “dwelling on the fact that hawks have taken two of our chickens this week and how our cow is sick—how things don’t seem like they could get much worse. I think maybe it’s time I start reading the Bible more often, too. What do you think?” Fritz’s smile spread across his whole face. “I think that’s a great idea.” Jakob gave a nod. “Well then, I will. But let’s get working. Today I’m painting the second clock we made. I think we’re going to beat the winter snow, Fritz, and get these clocks sold in town. I can’t really use your help today, but I would like you to follow along with me as I paint—just like we did yesterday. I’ll explain what I’m doing, and you practice the same thing on paper.” Fritz smiled. “OK!” “You know, Fritz, you could be a great disciple of Christ and a famous painter if you wanted to.” Fritz nodded. “You’re right.” As they worked, they talked. “So, are you going to tell your teacher about Günter stealing your poem?” Jakob asked. “Maybe, but not yet. It would be so much better if Günter decided to tell the truth.” “You really think he would?” “Well, I have been praying that he will. Elsie said that he has been different at school.” “In what way?” “Well, he isn’t being mean to her anymore—or anyone. She says he seems pretty depressed and moody, though.” “Hmm . . . You know, I learned a little about Günter from his uncle, Herr Becker, that I’ve been meaning to tell you about. Günter is an only child. His family is very wealthy, but his mother is sickly and his father travels a lot. He was sent to his uncle’s house because he’d been getting into trouble at school. They felt that the hard work of farm life could be good for Günter. Apparently, Günter has not enjoyed farm work at all, though. In fact, he doesn’t do it. He just sits around and carves wood when he should be working. Herr Becker wants to send him back home, but he said Günter’s father is not so nice to Günter. Poor Herr Becker; he feels stuck.” “That is interesting,” said Fritz. “Günter is done with school—today is his last day. I wonder what is going to happen to him.” “Me too,” echoed Jakob. After three hours of practicing painting, Fritz made a declaration as he held up his work. “Well, I think we were both wrong about me being a famous painter one day. And . . . I don’t think I want to be one anyway.” Jakob studied the painting that ended up looking nothing like it should. “Well, . . . yes, you have a way to go.” “I made no improvement from yesterday. Really, Father, were you this bad at painting when you started learning clockmaking?” “Not really. I actually took to it very quickly, but I’m sure you will learn over time.” Fritz sighed. “Listen, Fritz,” said Jakob. “I’ll finish painting this clock. Why don’t you go work on that story you’ve been itching to work on—The True Story of Fritz Vogel? I don’t think you’ve been able to write a word for weeks now.” “Really, Father?” “Yes. And give your mother a kiss for me!” “I will!” As Fritz made his way out of the workshop, he pondered the missing fifty pages of his book. I still can’t understand how they could be missing. I guess I’ll have to start over. Moving slowly with his crutches, he made his way to his little chest in the hallway by the parlor. There was his pen and his Bible, but they seemed a little out of place in the box. Something seemed a little odd. Then Fritz realized what it was—the missing pages of his story were back! “How odd!” exclaimed Fritz. He lifted the papers out of the chest and hugged them. “How wonderful!” After Fritz had settled down at the parlor table with his papers and pen, he looked out the window. Up on the hill, he saw something moving. He saw three things moving, in fact. Two girls, one with red hair, and a little white dog. “Peter!” Fritz cried loudly, “Peter! Come quickly!” Peter was too far away to hear him, but Fritz’s mother came running in. “What is it?” she cried. “Mother, look! Up on the hill, do you see the two girls and the dog?” She did see them—right before they disappeared over the crest of the hill. “Oh, Fritz! They’re the girls from your story, aren’t they? I can’t imagine where they live. We know all the farms around here, and no one has a red-headed girl.” “I know! It’s so strange. And, Mother, my story just appeared again. Do you—do you think those girls had anything to do with it?” “I can’t imagine how they could.” “I know, but the girls are so mysterious and so was the disappearance of my story. I really want to talk to them. Can I take Chocolate and try to find them?” His mother smiled knowingly. “Yes, Fritz, but try not to scare them if you do find them.” Unfortunately, it took Fritz longer than he wanted to get his horse ready. He searched the area thoroughly, crossing little streams and weaving through the forest, but there was no sign of the girls.
