A day after the funeral, yellow leaves twirled in the playful breeze, and one landed in Elsie’s hair as she rode with Fritz on his horse. “Fritz, I do hope that you win the contest! I’m so excited the winners are being announced today. I so want you to get your oil for the lantern.” “Elsie, thank you, but if I do win, I could never buy oil now. I must use the money to help Father. He talked to me this morning. Things are bad, Elsie. He used all the money we had to fix the roof and buy more hay for the animals. We have eggs, milk, and potatoes, but little else to tide us over the winter. The robbers took our cart and farm tools, and we have no money for seed next year. Father thinks we will likely lose the farmhouse in the spring—if we can even make it through the winter.” “I can’t believe it,” Elsie said, brushing some leaves from her hair. “Herr Engel would’ve helped us through the winter if he were here. What is going to happen to his house and his animals and things?” “We don’t know yet. Farmer Becker is helping us try to find out if he had any family. We haven’t been able to find a will or anything.” “I never heard him mention any family my whole life. He never visited any family or had any family visit that I can remember.” The schoolhouse was in sight now. Fritz felt a sudden ache in his throat. “Elsie, there’s something else. Today is my last day of school.” “What? We have almost four weeks left. You are so close to finishing. Why would you stop now?” “With the crops ruined and all the potatoes harvested, Father has already started clockmaking. He is hoping to get at least a couple of clocks made and taken to town before the winter locks us in our farmhouse. He needs some money for the basic things we need right now, like an ax to chop firewood, since the robbers took ours. I’m not much help with clockmaking yet, but I am going to do what I can.” “I understand,” said Elsie. “Oh, I hope more than ever that you win the competition.” The whole school was so excited about the competition that the teacher decided to announce the winners first thing. “As you know,” he began, “there are two winners. The first-place winner receives twenty marks, and the winning poem has already been entered into the regional competition. The second-place winner receives a brand-new dictionary. I will now announce the first-place winner. Please remember that the competition was judged by a colleague of mine who does not know any of you. He actually works for a publisher and has a great eye for good writing. I completely trust his judgment.” The room was ready to burst with anticipation. The teacher picked up a poem from off his desk. “First place goes to Günter Wolf.” The room gasped and then was silent. There was no clapping. Günter slunk down in his chair a little. “Günter,” said the teacher, “I would like to have you come up and read your winning poem.” Günter’s face turned red. “Oh . . . I didn’t know that the winning poem would be read to everyone. I . . . really don’t want to share it.” “I insist, Günter. It’s a beautiful poem. You have a real gift, and it is such a lovely tribute to our beautiful Black Forest,” replied the teacher. Günter hung his head and shook it. “Very well, I will read it to the class myself.” The teacher began reading as Günter slunk down even further in his chair. To Be a Tree in the Black Forest By Günter Wolf To be a tree in the Black Forest Now, that would be quite the thing. To be brushed by ferns and clothed with moss To house the singing birds and make friends with a gurgling stream. That’s my poem! The words screamed in Fritz’s mind as the teacher continued to read the poem. Fritz knew exactly what had happened. When the poems blew away, thanks to Günter, Günter had disappeared behind the schoolhouse. He grabbed a poem that was blowing away and then copied it to another paper with his own name as the author, Fritz thought. He looked over at Günter. The boy was hanging his head with his eyes closed. Oh, God, prayed Fritz. What should I do? Fritz would not have had a problem standing up and letting the teacher know that it was his poem. It was not right for Günter to win. But something spoke to Fritz’s heart to be still and to wait quietly. So, he did not say anything, even though he felt like shouting. After the poem was read, a murmur of soft voices moved through the classroom as the teacher gave Günter the twenty marks: “Can you believe it?” “That was beautiful.” “I would never have guessed Günter could write poetry,” was whispered. “Silence, please, class. I have the second winner to announce.” Many pairs of anxious eyes focused their attention back on the teacher. “Second place goes to Fritz Vogel.” The room burst into applause. Fritz didn’t know how to feel. So many emotions were swirling in his mind. He found himself at the front of the room, reading the poem slowly, trying to control his emotions. I Cannot Hate By Fritz Vogel There is one who has mistreated me, Who cares nothing for my tender heart, And the dreams that I have held so close He has sought to tear apart. But he, a beloved child of God, I cannot hate nor despise, For though his actions bring me pain, He is precious in God’s eyes, A face and hands that God hath formed, A beating heart that God holds near, I cannot hate the man because God considers him so dear. The room was still. It was not the kind of poem everyone had been expecting. Suddenly, there was the scrape of a chair and footsteps running to the door. Günter had fled the classroom.
