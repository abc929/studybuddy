For a glorious week, Roman had visited the Vogel farm. Now, Fritz and Elsie sat by the garden and watched the carriage carrying Roman and his family as it wound down the road and disappeared around the corner. “We will all miss him,” stated Fritz. “Yes, we will. So what did he think of farm life and a big family, Fritz? Did he tell you?” Elsie asked. “Yes, he did. He loved being around a big family. He didn’t love farm work, however.” “Really?” “Yeah. He loved talking with Peter in the fields, but his muscles ached, and his hands were full of blisters. He helped with a cheerful attitude, though, and said he had a new appreciation for farmers.” “It was nice of you to give Roman your story, Fritz,” Elsie commented. “He really wanted it. To be honest, I was sad to give it away.” Fritz let out a sigh. “I’m writing another story, though.” “Oh? What about?” “It was Herr Engel’s idea. It’s my story about my life. It’s just for me, and maybe my kids someday. But Roman’s story about the nun and the abbey—that really sparked some story ideas.” “What did Roman’s father say about the abbey ruins?” “Well, there were over a dozen walnut trees. They dug around a little, but ultimately they just spent the week enjoying the hot springs and hiking trails in the area.” A flash of blue came flying out the door. Flora dashed to Fritz and settled in his lap, smoothing her blue dress around her. “It’s Fritz and Flora time,” she declared. Fritz patted her head of tight blonde curls. “Yes, it is! Today, I have a surprise for you.” He picked up the bouquet of flowers lying beside him and presented them to the delighted girl. “I had Roman pick these before he left. Today, I’m going to tell you a story about a prince who was given a quest to find a certain flower before the sun set. It’s one of the types of flowers in your bouquet.” Flora clapped her hands together. “Which one?” “You’ll find out in the story.” Twenty minutes later, Jakob appeared with Fritz’s braces and crutches. “Run in to your mother, Flora, dear. Are you ready, Fritz?” “You’re not going to give up, are you, Father? I didn’t even get a break when Roman was here.” “I know it’s hard, son, but you are progressing. You couldn’t stand up for more than two seconds, and now you can stand up for four or five seconds!” “At this rate, I should be taking a couple steps within just a few short years,” Fritz laughed. “Thank you, Father. Actually, I really am glad you are pushing me.” “Wake up, Fritz.” Fritz stirred, sat up, and saw Peter in the dim light handing him a stack of clothes. “Oh, it’s time!” exclaimed Fritz. He dressed as fast as he could. “Elsie is going to take your school bag and bring your lunch when she comes later,” said Peter. ttt Fritz was about to put on his boots, but he stopped. “I don’t think I’ll wear my boots, Peter— just my socks. The boots are pretty heavy.” “You are going to go to school in your socks?” “You bet! I want to be as light as I can be for you. And what do I need the boots for anyway?” “Aren’t you worried about what the other kids will think of you?” “Not at all.” Peter smiled. “OK, then. I guess we should try anything that will help.” He got on his knees and crouched down so that Fritz could get on his back. Fritz was quite sure he heard Peter groan as he struggled a bit to stand up. This is not going to work, Fritz thought as they left the house, his heart sinking. The world was quiet. Fritz didn’t want Peter to get more winded by talking, so he remained silent. It was barely bright enough for Peter to see his way. Fritz was grateful for the cool air of a late spring morning, but he wondered how hot it would be on the way home—or worse, how hot it would be in the summer. Here in the forest, the school schedule was designed to meet the needs of the people. It was too hard to get to school in the winter snow, and everyone in the family was needed for harvest time at the end of the summer, so school was held at other times. Silently, Peter trudged down the hill, across the small bridge, and into the forest. When the forest path started to incline, Fritz heard Peter’s heavy breathing. They slowed down significantly, Peter barely plodding along. Fritz’s voice broke the heavy silence. “Peter, I think we should go back.” “No,” came the single-word answer in a labored voice. Just as Peter and Fritz were coming out of the forest, a third of the way to the school, the first rays of morning sun burst forth from over the hills. Golden light poured into the valley, and the birds, which had been twittering here and there, now couldn’t contain their merry singing. The world was alive and awake. Peter stopped by a large rock. “Fritz, I’m going to set you down on the rock. I . . . just need . . . a break.” Peter’s breathing was heavy. Sitting on the rock, Fritz watched as Peter stood bent over, his hands on his knees. Sweat lined the hair on his forehead. “I won’t go any further, Peter,” Fritz said. “I can’t. Please . . . let’s go home.” Peter didn’t look up or answer. “Peter, we’re only a third of the way there. And even if we make it, you have to run all the way back home. And then . . . do it all over after school? It’s just too much. You tried . . . I’m so grateful. I can’t even explain to you what this means to me.” Peter was still leaning over. His shoulders shook. Peter is crying. I can’t remember him ever crying. Fritz’s eyes filled with tears too. Peter looked up. “Fritz, I can’t bear what has happened to you. I just can’t. It’s not fair. I can’t stand it if you don’t go to school.” He sat down on the cool grass and hung his head. “But you’re right. I can’t do it. I don’t even know if I can get you back home from here. I’m afraid we’ll have to take a lot of breaks.” An hour later, Elsie and the girls were on their way to school when they met Peter and Fritz in the forest. “You’re going the wrong way,” said Elsie. Then she quickly saw that Peter’s eyes were rimmed in red and that his shirt was soaked with sweat. “Oh, Peter, let me help you!” she cried. “No, Elsie,” came the labored response. “Go on ahead to school.” When the boys arrived home, morning was almost over. Jakob insisted that Peter take the day off. There was not an “I told you so” from Jakob, just private tears of his own—tears of pride in his boys, and tears of sorrow for their trials. After a hearty, late lunch, Peter and Fritz cooled off in the river. Fritz had already gained a lot of upper-body strength and was able to easily pull himself in and out of the water. In an area the family had dug into the riverbank years ago, the cool, clear water was waist deep and still. Fritz floated on his back, gazing into the leafy canopy above him, and Peter did the same, while the little white dog curled up for a nap on the grassy riverbank. “Peter, I know you never wanted to work at Hotel Zum. Why didn’t you ever say anything? Why did you just go along with it, even though you were unhappy?” Peter continued to gaze up into the trees as he considered the question. “Well, I don’t think you want to be a clockmaker, right? Why do you not say anything about it?” “That’s a good point. I don’t really have a choice. I can see that you didn’t really have a choice either. But do you think you could’ve been happy working at the hotel?” “I don’t know. It sure didn’t feel like it, even though I kept trying to convince myself that I could like it. Do you think you can be happy as a clockmaker?” “It doesn’t feel like it. I don’t know why, but it just holds no interest for me. Father says each clock is a work of art, but the clock-making process looks so tedious.” “What about painting the clock faces? Remember how Father said that’s how Karl Hofer got his start?” “Yeah, I know. Maybe I will love the painting. If I could paint some scenes from this forest, I would like that. I love this beautiful forest.” Just then, the little dog awoke with a start and began barking wildly. Fritz and Peter looked around and didn’t see anything. Leaping into the air, the dog splashed into the river and swam across. “What’s going on?” exclaimed Fritz as he pulled himself up onto the grassy bank to get a better view of what the dog was doing. Having reached the other side, the dog bounded through the bushes. “It’s them!” cried Fritz, pointing to the forest. “It’s them!” “It’s who?” asked Peter, joining Fritz on the bank. “The girls! The girls at the tree with the door. I just saw them. The little white dog is theirs.” “Where are they?” “The girl with the red hair picked up the dog, and then she and her little sister—I think it’s her sister—ran off into the forest. Go, Peter! Go see if you can stop them. I need to find out who they are!” “Without my shoes and shirt?” “Yes! Go, please!” Peter dove into the river and swam as fast as he could. “Which way?” he called after he had reached the other side and pulled himself up the bank. “Straight ahead!” Fritz waited for fifteen minutes, each minute seeming endless. Finally, Peter appeared through the forest, looking defeated. He swam back across the narrow river and joined Fritz on the bank. “I couldn’t see any sign of them. I was slow because of my bare feet, I guess. I’m sorry, Fritz.” Fritz patted his brother on the back. “Thank you for trying. I can’t figure out where those girls are from. This is not close to where that tree was. It’s all so . . . baffling.” They sat on the bank in silence for a couple of minutes, each boy lost in his own thoughts. Then Peter picked up a fishing pole he had brought. “Should we see if we can catch some trout for dinner?” Forty minutes later, a rainbow trout was in their bucket and they were fishing for another. In the distance, they saw their sisters coming home from school just across the bridge. “Hello!” Fritz said, raising his hand in a hearty welcome. What a joyful sight it was to see his sisters with their bouncing braids and colorful clothes. “Elsie! Come on over!” Peter called. Elsie had her head down and plodded up the hill toward the farmhouse. Amalia and Agatha were on either side of Elsie, holding onto her arms. Fritz squinted, trying to figure out what was going on. Spirited Gretchen marched over to the boys. She reached them a little out of breath, but squared her shoulders before speaking. “Well, it has been quite a horrid day. It’s all Günter Wolf’s fault.” “Who’s Günter Wolf?” asked Fritz, noticing that Gretchen’s face was extremely dirty. “Oh, just the meanest boy who ever did live. He’s a new boy at school, and he made fun of Elsie. He followed us part of the way home, just laughing at us. Then he handed Elsie a folded-up paper. It had a mean picture on it that said, ‘One-Eyed Elsie.’” Gretchen flopped onto the grassy bank with her brothers. “Poor Elsie! She told us on the way to school that she was worried about what people would think of her wounded eye and the scar across her face. That Günter made her feel so bad! Well, do you know what I did? I ran after him and kicked him as hard as I could!” “You did?” said Peter in surprise. “Gretchen!” “Well, I was just so mad! And then . . . and then . . . well, he grabbed a handful of dirt and smeared it all over my face.” Fritz’s heart was beating hard. “How could someone do that to Elsie and to you?” “I hate him!” Gretchen declared. Fritz took a deep breath. “No, Gretchen.” Fritz said a prayer in his mind that God would calm his heart. “Never hate anyone.” “But, Elsie cried the whole way home!” retorted Gretchen. “I’ve never seen Elsie cry like that. She could hardly even see through her tears, so Agatha and Amalia had to hold her arms the whole way so she didn’t trip.” “I know,” said Fritz. “What he did was so very wrong, but the Bible teaches us to love our enemies.” “Love Günter Wolf?” Gretchen questioned with raised eyebrows. “Yes,” said Fritz. “I had heard that Farmer Becker’s nephew was coming to live with him—a boy my age. That must be Günter. I have an idea. Let’s get home, and I’ll tell you all about it.” Fritz buckled on the knee pads that his father had made him. Fritz could crawl really well, and the pads helped protect his knees. “Go on ahead, Peter. Check on Elsie. I won’t be too far behind you.”
