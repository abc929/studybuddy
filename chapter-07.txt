“Fritz.” It was Elsie’s voice. A story of heart-wrenching sorrow was told in that one word. There was no answer. Fritz did not turn his head toward the sound, even though this was the first time Elsie had ventured to see him since the accident. He continued to stare at the glazed, green stove tiles. For three weeks he had not asked about Elsie. For three weeks he had lain still in the parlor, sinking deeper into despair and wondering, What if Elsie had just not climbed so high up in that tree? “Fritz, I—I’m sorry. Oh, I’m so sorry! Dear Fritz, oh what have I done to you? How can you ever forgive me?” Hot tears stung Fritz’s eyes. The remorse in Elsie’s voice stirred his heart, but he could not bring himself to forgive her. How foolish—how horribly foolish you were, Elsie! You have ruined my life! You should never have climbed so high, he thought as anger coursed through him. He kept his head turned so Elsie would not see the tears spilling out of his eyes. It’s Elsie’s fault that my hip is broken and one of my feet is broken and the other is crushed. It’s Elsie’s fault that I’ll never walk normally again! “Fritz . . . please.” Elsie’s voice trembled, and a sob escaped her. Herr Engel, who had just stopped by, stood in the doorway and watched as Fritz remained silent, not turning his head to see his sister. Elsie fled the room, and Herr Engel took a seat at the parlor table. The chair scraped against the floor as he pulled it out. “You are at a fork in the road, Fritz,” Herr Engel said in his kindly voice. “I’ve been there.” Fritz kept his head turned toward the stove, not sure what Herr Engel was talking about and not caring at all. “I’m tired. I just want to be alone.” “It was a fever,” began Herr Engel, ignoring Fritz’s request. “My wife Emilia got it first. She was the most lovely woman this world could know—always laughing, always singing. She was everything to me. I watched the life pass out of her, and on the same day, death took our baby girl with little black curls and soft cheeks. The next day, death took our bouncing three-year￾old boy, Jürgen, and our precious five-year-old, Hans, who thought he was a man like me. They were my world. I was suddenly alone—terribly and utterly alone. I dug four graves behind our farmhouse. Their bodies have lain there for the past thirty-seven years. I know devastation. I know pain like you are feeling, Fritz.” Fritz finally turned his head to look at the man. “I . . . never knew.” This new revelation lightened Fritz’s own despair for a moment, and he studied his neighbor. Though wrinkled and framed with white hair, Herr Engel’s face had been shaped by years of cheer and joy, and his eyes sparkled with life. “But you are so happy. You’re the most cheerful person I know.” “I was not happy for a while, though, Fritz. I thought my life was over,” admitted Herr Engel, “just as you do now. But then something happened to me, and at that fork in the road, I chose a different path.” Fritz swallowed hard but could not keep back the tears. “The doctor said I’ll never walk again without crutches, and even then it will be painful and slow and awkward. One of my feet will always be quite twisted, and my right hip broke so badly that it won’t ever work quite right again, making it even harder to walk.” Herr Engel listened. “And how do you feel about all that, Fritz?” “I feel . . . terrible. I’m so angry! Why did this happen to me? Why did Elsie do something so foolish? It all seems like a bad dream. It can’t be real. But it is! And now . . . it’s hard to hope for anything. I was going to be something great someday. I was going to do something big in this world. And now I can’t do anything. What kind of life can I have?” There was noise outside the door, and then Fritz’s father walked in with the schoolteacher, who took off his scarf. Oh! How could things get any worse? I do not want my teacher to see me like this, and with a tear-streaked face. I’ve never been so humiliated! The visit was short. The teacher stood up, pulled out a paper, and cleared his throat. “I’ve prepared this for you. As you know, school is compulsory, but as Fritz is now disabled, he is excused from school attendance. We will miss you, Fritz. I’m very sorry.” Disabled? “I want to finish school,” said Fritz quietly, knowing as he spoke that it was not possible. “We have no horse, no wagon,” said Father sorrowfully. “The walk is over an hour. There is no way for you to get to school, Fritz.” This new revelation was the last straw, and Fritz fell into a deeper depression, despite everyone’s best efforts to cheer him up. Over the next two weeks, his mother could hardly get him to eat. Elsie came to the parlor twice, but Fritz kept his head turned away from her and remained silent. She felt so bad that she didn’t even eat with the family in the parlor. Finally, Fritz could handle it no more and asked for Herr Engel. “Tell me about the fork in the road,” pleaded Fritz, almost as soon as the man had arrived and seated himself by the fireplace. “I thought that’s why you asked me to come,” said Herr Engel. “I’m glad. Well, one road is despair, and it is a dark, gloomy, depressing road, indeed. You need not choose that road anymore. There is another road for you, Fritz, one filled with light and laughter and love. It is a road so full of hope and power and strength that no trial can take away the light and joy you feel. I found that road, and, despite overwhelming trials, I have found that life is miraculously beautiful and joyful. That road is the one where Jesus Christ helps you every step of the way, comforting you, strengthening you, watching over you, and bringing you joy.” Jesus Christ. Light. Hope. Strength. Joy. Fritz clung to the words that shone a beam of light through the oppressing darkness he felt. The elderly man pulled his chair closer to Fritz’s bedside, opened the Bible he had brought, and for an hour read very slowly, flipping around to different verses that he knew well. Fritz closed his eyes and felt peace for the first time since the accident. We are troubled on every side, yet not distressed; we are perplexed, but not in despair . . . cast down, but not destroyed; Be not afraid, neither be thou dismayed: for the Lord thy God is with thee whithersoever thou goest. The Lord is my light and my salvation; whom shall I fear? the Lord is the strength of my life; Be strong and of a good courage, fear not, nor be afraid . . . for the Lord thy God . . . will not fail thee, nor forsake thee. Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls. For my yoke is easy, and my burden is light. Fritz had heard these words before when his father read the Bible to the family each Sabbath day, but now Fritz seemed to be hearing them for the first time. They were both powerful and sweet to his soul. He was desperate for more of these words. Herr Engel seemed to understand. He set the Bible on Fritz’s bed. “This is yours now—my gift to you. I have my late wife’s Bible at home. I want you to have mine. Now, listen carefully, Fritz. A casual, half-hearted relationship with Christ will not be enough. You must come to know Him and feel Him with you. His words must always be in your heart, your thoughts, your actions, and your words. And also, nothing will really help you until you forgive Elsie. She has always supported you in every trial you’ve had. She never meant to harm you, and she is devastated right now. Now, she needs you to support her in her trials.” Her trials . . . her trials? I—I suppose it must feel terrible for her to carry the guilt of causing my accident. Fritz thought about how much he really needed Elsie. He always had. He also was not one to hold on to grudges. He did want Elsie to be happy again, just like he wanted to be happy again himself. And forgiving her would be one less horrible burden for them both to bear. With these thoughts, he suddenly found he couldn’t wait to forgive his sister. “Can you please send for her, Herr Engel?” Fritz had always been facing the other way when Elsie was in the room, so when she walked through the door and stood looking at him with a trembling lower lip, Fritz gasped softly. “Your face, Elsie!” “I know. A branch hit it as I fell. I was able to grab another branch to prevent falling further, but this was already done.” She touched her face lightly. A long, almost-healed wound started at her forehead, went across her eye and cheek, and ended at her upper jaw. She touched her right eye. “I’m blind in this eye.” “Oh, Elsie! I’m so sorry! And I’m so sorry for not forgiving you. I forgive you with all my heart.” “I’m the one who’s sorry,” said Elsie, kneeling by Fritz’s bed and laying her head on his shoulder. “Thank you for forgiving me. Oh, Fritz, what can I do? I can never change what has happened to you.” Fritz smiled. “I know two things you can do.” “Anything!” Elsie cried. “You need to be your cheerful self again. I need you. Feeling bad about what happened will only make it harder—for both of us.” “I will try, Fritz!” said Elsie, smiling despite the tears streaming down her face. “What is the other thing?” Fritz held up the Bible. “Will you read this with me every day? And I mean every single day. Let’s not miss a day—ever.” “A Bible?” Elsie looked confused. “Herr Engel gave it to me. I need it, Elsie. I need these words. Despair—it wants to wrap around me. It’s suffocating. It’s dark. These words—they are life and light. Read with me every day.” “Yes, yes, yes, Fritz! Of course I will.” Elsie stayed with Fritz that entire day. They laughed. They cried. Elsie could not be separated from him until it was so late that Father insisted she go to her bed. Brother and sister were reunited.
