“Do good to them that hate you,” said Jakob after he, Maria, Elsie, and Gretchen had returned from the Becker Farm and gathered with the rest of the family in the parlor. “Fritz, there is great wisdom in those words, and I’m glad you reminded us of them.” “How did it go?” asked Fritz eagerly, looking at Elsie. “You were there for a long time, it seems.” Elsie untied her hat, smiling. “I don’t think anyone except you, Fritz Vogel, could convince me to go and bring a jar of our best honey to welcome Günter as our new neighbor.” “How did he react?” asked Fritz. Jakob laughed. “Oh, Fritz! The poor boy! He looked so scared. I’m sure he thought we had come because of what he did to Elsie and Gretchen. He seemed very confused when Elsie handed him the honey with a very kind smile.” “Well, I didn’t smile at him,” piped in Gretchen. “But I didn’t kick him again, at least.” “I’m glad you didn’t kick him,” replied Fritz with a little laugh, “but Gretchen, what do you think of making it a goal to smile at him tomorrow at school? Can you do that for me?” “That would be really hard for me. No one should be mean to Elsie.” Maria gestured for Gretchen to come sit by her. “You know, Gretchen, you are a strong and determined little girl. I was reading the Bible this morning, and I read about prayer. You could ask God to help you use that strength and determination to smile at Günter. God can help you do anything, even smile at someone who was unkind to you and your sister.” Gretchen nodded. “I will try.” Jakob clapped his hands. “Fritz, I can’t wait another moment to show you something! What great timing it was to go to the Beckers’ farm. There was something there for you, and we brought it home. That’s why it took a little extra time to get back.” Jakob whispered in Peter’s ear. Then the two of them picked up Fritz and carried him out of the parlor. “What do you mean?” asked Fritz as they headed down the hall. “You’ll see,” said Jakob. A minute later, Jakob and Peter set Fritz down on a bench in the stables. There, directly in front of Fritz, was a dark chestnut-brown horse with a long golden-brown mane and a patch of white running from his forehead to his nose. “He’s your horse,” said Jakob quietly. Fritz’s heart soared as he looked into the horse’s large brown eyes. Fritz felt an immediate bond with the animal. It was as if the years ahead flashed before him, and he knew that he and the horse would be loyal and true friends. “He’s . . . my horse? I don’t understand.” “Someone bought him for you,” answered Jakob. “It wasn’t me, but I can’t tell you who it is. That person wants to remain anonymous. Elsie and Gretchen here don’t even know, so you don’t need to ask them.” “I—I can’t believe it. I really can’t believe it! The horse is beautiful! He looks just like the color of chocolate, and that’s what I’ll call him. Peter, could you get my crutches for me so that I can stand up by the horse? I want to brush him,” Fritz asked with breathless excitement. Peter left to get them, and Fritz turned to his father and said, “I can go to school now, can’t I?” “Yes, but not right away. The school is not close. I want you to practice riding close by for a while first. It might take you some time to master getting on and off the horse by yourself. Also, you will need to gain some more strength in your leg muscles.” “That all makes sense, Father. But—wow!—I can’t figure out who gave this horse to me. I mean . . . do you know how much a horse costs? It must have been Herr Engel. But could he even afford a horse? Or maybe it was Roman? I did tell Roman about wanting a horse.” Father smiled. “Obviously, it was someone who cares about you. That’s all I can say.” ttt The next day, the cloudless blue sky, the waving wildflowers on the hills, and the merry birds flitting to and fro all matched Fritz’s joyful mood. Jakob had arranged for Herr Becker to come over in the morning and give Fritz some training on riding a horse. Fritz then spent two hours riding around the farm. Jakob did not forget about his son’s daily practice with his crutches, and Fritz took his first steps without his father holding him. Maria came out to see. The steps were slow and awkward, but before practice ended, he had taken five or six steps with his crutches. Life seemed beautiful for Fritz. Elsie, on the other hand, had not experienced such a good day. It was Gretchen, again, who told Fritz and Peter the story when the girls got home from school. “Well, today was even worse than yesterday! When Elsie sat down on her chair, what do you think she sat on? Honey! A whole jar of it had been poured out on her chair. She didn’t see it, and she was a sticky mess all day.” “Oh, no!” said Fritz. “Our teacher was so mad,” said Gretchen. “No one admitted to doing it. Of course it was Günter, but no one saw him do it, so he didn’t get in trouble. But I did what you said, Fritz, and I smiled at him. I smiled at him so hard that my cheeks hurt, but my teacher didn’t think it was a smile. He told me to stop scowling at Günter.” “Gretchen, I’m proud of you for trying to smile at him,” replied Fritz. “Yeah, well. I don’t think he liked it. On the way home from school, a mud ball came flying from the bushes and hit me in the back. Look at this!” Gretchen turned around, and the back of her dress had three big mud splatters on it. “Three times a mud ball hit me. I know it was Günter, but I couldn’t see him, so there is no way to get him in trouble. What are we going to do?” Fritz thought for a moment. “There must be a reason why Günter is being mean. I wish we knew what it was; then maybe we could help him. I’m so sorry, Gretchen. I’ll practice riding more so I can come to school sooner and see what I can do to help. How is Elsie?” “She didn’t say anything the whole way home. She’s weeding the garden.” Fritz made his way to the garden, again grateful for the knee pads Jakob had made for him. While crawling around was tiresome and awkward, he was hopeful that walking with his crutches would get easier and easier. Fritz found Elsie weeding the herb garden. He knelt beside her and began weeding too. “Gretchen told me about the honey, Elsie. I’m so sorry.” “Me too.” Elsie sighed and stopped weeding. When she turned to look his way, Fritz noticed a tear run down her cheek, and then his twin’s chin crumpled up as if she was going to cry even more. “I know he’s just being mean,” she said, choking back the tears. “The other kids are kind to me. But it still hurts . . . because, well, I just don’t feel pretty anymore. No one’s going to want to marry me now—not with this scar across my face and my eye looking all funny.” “Elsie, I don’t think that’s true.” “I wish I could believe that.” “I wish you could, too,” declared Fritz. “Don’t let Günter’s actions make you lose confidence in yourself.” Her mouth twisted unhappily. “You think I should still be kind to him, don’t you?” “I do. Think of kind things you can do. Never be mean back. Even if it doesn’t change him . . . it will change you. We may need Father to talk to Günter’s uncle if this keeps up, though.” “Oh, Fritz!” exclaimed Elsie, suddenly cheerful. “I forgot to tell you something exciting. We started studying poetry today at school, and our teacher announced a poetry competition for our school. You can enter up to ten poems, and you have all the way until the end of August to write the poems. There will be two winning poems. The first-place poem receives twenty marks as a reward, and it is entered into the regional competition. The second-place poem receives a brand-new dictionary! Oh, Fritz, I just know you could win the competition! The story you gave to Roman was written so beautifully. And I loved the poem you gave to Mother about the clouds.” “Oh, that was just a little poem, but Elsie, this does make me excited. Twenty marks! Wow! I could buy a can of lantern oil that could last me an entire year!” “Lantern oil? That’s what you would buy?” “Oh, I’m thinking of the winter when I’ll have to be working on clocks all day. It gets dark so early. There will be no light for writing my story after work or even for getting up early to read the Bible. The daylight hours are so short in the winter. I know Mother and Father don’t have extra oil.” “Well, Fritz Vogel, I hope you win that competition!”
