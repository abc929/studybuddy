The rays of late morning sunshine fought their way weakly through a cloud-checkered sky. Fritz and Elsie worked side by side harvesting carrots from the garden, while partway up the hill, Amalia and Agatha were playing with Gretchen and little Flora. “I’m to keep my eye on Flora,” Elsie told Fritz. “Don’t let me forget.” Peter came around the side of the house with a wooden cart. “Father said to put the carrots in here, Fritz. We need to work quickly. He thinks winter is coming early this year. We’ve got a lot of harvesting to get done.” Fritz stood up. “Are you mad at me, Peter— about yesterday?” “No.” “Are you sure? I’m really sorry.” “Yes, Fritz, I’m sure,” said Peter, before turning and retreating behind the house. “I don’t understand Peter,” Fritz told Elsie. “It seems like he has been so somber and quiet all school year. I mean, he has always been quiet, but he seems so subdued now. I am not even sure if he has feelings. He doesn’t seem to get angry or excited or happy or sad about anything. I wish we could be better friends.” “You’re so cheerful and fun, Fritz. I don’t see how you couldn’t be friends with anyone. Why aren’t you better friends with Peter?” “I don’t know. I wish I knew.” A light, misty rain started to fall just as they heard Amalia shout across the hill, “Flora’s running to the river! I’m sorry! I didn’t realize she had left.” Elsie and Fritz dashed down the hill and easily caught up with Flora well before she reached the river that wound through the meadow below their home. Elsie swooped the daring two-year-old up into her arms. “Let’s go back to the girls!” she said. A movement from down in the valley caught Fritz’s eye. “Our cow!” he shouted to Elsie. “She must have gone through the shallow part of the river, and she’s wandered off into the valley. I’ve got to go get her.” The sky above Fritz had turned to a solid sheet of white that seemed to be lowering itself onto the tips of the treetops and wrapping itself around the hills. “Fog!” said Fritz, now thoroughly soaked by the misty rain. He knew how thick the fog could get in this area. By the time Fritz got to the cow, the heavy fog had settled into the valley like a wet quilt. Fritz couldn’t see more than five paces ahead. He hesitated. “Well, Nelly,” he said, patting the cow, “if we keep heading straight ahead, we’ll hear the river in just a couple of minutes and follow its sound. Home is just on the other side of the river.” Much to his surprise, Fritz didn’t hear the river after walking a full ten minutes. He didn’t hear anything but his own footsteps and the lumbering steps of the cow. Even the birds were quiet. Fritz stopped. He could see the faint form of thick tree trunks. “We’re in the woods now, Nelly. This isn’t right. Let’s go back.” With waning confidence, Fritz turned around. Tree trunks kept appearing out of the mist, however, and Fritz quickly knew he was lost. The forests around his home were thick and uninhabited for kilometers on end. Nelly refused to go any farther and began to graze. No coaxing or pulling could get the hungry cow to leave the patch of grass she had found, so Fritz sat down under a tree where the ground was free from dew. He leaned back against the tree, hoping the fog would dissipate soon. The night before, Fritz had slept terribly, kept awake by his swirling thoughts and worries about what life held for him. Now his eyelids closed, and before long, Fritz dozed off. A flutter of wings in the trees above him eventually woke him. He sat up and rubbed his eyes. As he looked around, two realizations flashed into his mind: the fog was gone . . . and so was Nelly! Just as he was about to shout for Nelly, he heard voices nearby— the voices of girls. Turning his head toward the sound, he saw through the foliage two girls, one about his age and one several years younger. A small, white dog bounced along beside them. Fritz watched as the girls stopped in front of a huge oak tree with heavy, twisting branches. It really was a beautiful tree in a clearing strewn with large, moss-covered rocks. The girl Fritz’s age had red hair, something Fritz had never seen. She tucked a long, curly wisp of hair back into her white scarf and spoke to the younger girl. “I’m sure he’s been here,” she said. Fritz watched in utter fascination as the older girl reached up to the tree and . . . what? It was like there was a little door in the tree—like a cupboard door. The girl opened it, took out a large white bundle, and placed it in her basket. Then she placed a paper inside the tree and closed the door. Fritz realized he was holding his breath. He let it out slowly and quietly. “Let’s see what it is this time,” said the younger girl eagerly, her brown braids bouncing. “No,” said the red-headed girl. “Let’s open it when we get home. We should hurry; it looks like it might start raining again.” As soon as they left, Fritz walked quietly to the tree. Sure enough, there was a little door with a knob made out of a slice of wood. No one would ever notice it just passing by. Carefully, Fritz opened the door. The hollowed-out area inside the tree held nothing but the note the girl had left. Fritz took the note, unfolded it, and read the handwritten words: We haven’t found the money yet, but we will keep looking. Thank you for your help. We are lonely, but we are well. Until we can meet again! Lisette and Little Heidi Lisette and Little Heidi? He put the paper back into the tree and closed the door. Astonishing! What could this mean? I have to find out. I have to find those girls! Completely forgetting about his cow, Fritz ran in the direction the girls had gone. When he saw a flash of color from their clothes through the distant trees, he yelled. “Hey! Wait! I want to talk to you!” The girls looked back, saw Fritz, and started to sprint away into the forest, but the little dog turned and ran toward Fritz, yapping up a storm. When the dog reached Fritz, it started nipping at Fritz’s heels. “No! Hey, don’t do that,” said Fritz, slowing down and shaking the dog from his boot. The dog kept yapping and following him, his little silver bell dinging wildly around his neck. After a couple of minutes, the dog finally left Fritz alone and bounded away back to the girls. Fritz followed the girls, every now and then catching a glimpse of them weaving in and out of the trees. Eventually, however, he lost sight of them completely. Fritz stopped and caught his breath. The wind had picked up in the forest, making a deep, mighty rushing sound as it rocked the tops of the trees. Looking up through the trees, he was grateful the fog was gone, but Fritz wasn’t too happy about the dark, angry patches of sky he saw. Think! said Fritz in his mind. Trees were everywhere. There was no path, and he had no idea where he was. The thought of his missing cow made him groan. His family depended on that cow for milk and butter for themselves; his mother sold the extra butter to their neighbor to earn a little money. What do I do now? Fritz stood still and tried to think. Listening carefully, he heard the faint sound of water. “The river!” he cried. There were many rivers crisscrossing the Black Forest, but maybe, just maybe, this was the river that ran past his home. Following the comforting sound of gently rushing water, Fritz found the river quickly and took long drinks of the clear, cool water. Standing up, he made a plan. This river is running downhill, and I’ve been running downhill all this time. If I go up the river, it might lead me home. Terribly hungry, cold, and wet, with clothes still damp from the morning rain and fog, Fritz began a steady jog alongside the riverbank. After an entire hour of jogging, Fritz suddenly stopped. The river had not looked familiar at all, and he was sure that he hadn’t been this far from home. Fritz, always diligent in saying his nightly prayers but hardly ever praying otherwise, knelt down and asked God for help. Getting up, he felt no guidance. He had no idea what to do. He didn’t doubt that God heard him; his father had instilled in him a deep trust in God. He remembered his father once saying, “Fritz, God hears every single prayer we say, and He always cares and wants to help us, but He often answers our prayers in His own way. It may not be how or when we want, but you can be sure—He will always help you.” Fritz hung on to those words and made a decision. I’m just going to keep following this river. Then at least I know I won’t be going in circles. Alternating between jogging and walking, Fritz went on for what seemed to be hours. At one point the forest opened up into a small meadow through which the river meandered, and Fritz was grateful to be out of the dark forest and into brighter light for a little bit. But the whole meadow was surrounded by forest, and Fritz had to reenter the dark thicket once again. As the light in the forest grew noticeably dimmer, Fritz spotted something welcoming: a patch of wild blueberry bushes. Ravenously, he ate handfuls of the ripe, juicy berries. Glancing around, Fritz’s heart suddenly leapt. He saw a narrow pathway leading farther into the forest. I’ll take a human-made pathway over this river any day, Fritz thought as he trotted onto the pathway and picked up his speed. The path led in a pretty straight line through the forest, though many parts seemed quite overgrown and faint. At times he even lost sight of the path altogether, but he was able to pick it up again. The wind had strengthened and blew wildly, bending the tree branches and whipping Fritz’s hair and clothes. Rain pelted the exhausted boy and soaked him once again, this time with big, splashing drops. Fritz pressed forward, going as fast as he could. The path grew a little wider, and a little wider, and then . . . it stopped. It was a dead end. Fritz stood at the end of the path, aghast and breathing heavily. He knew many old woodcutter paths led to nowhere, but he had hoped this was a different kind of path. By this time it was growing dark alarmingly fast. It suddenly dawned on Fritz that he was going to have to spend the night in the forest. Oh, how worried my family must be about me and Nelly. He shivered and longed for the comfort of his farmhouse. Fritz’s mission turned from trying to find his way home to finding a shelter from the storm—a place where he could spend the night. After trotting a short while through the forest, he found a large pine tree with far-spreading low branches. So thick were the pine needles that once Fritz squeezed his way under the branches, he found the ground completely dry and that only little wisps of wind broke through the layers of the branches. A growl in his stomach reminded Fritz of his hunger. He turned his focus to gathering up dry pine needles that lay all around. Eventually, he had a huge pile of the brown needles, and as the last light of day faded, he burrowed down into the pile of needles. They were poky but offered some padding and insulation from the cold, hard ground and kept some warmth around him. With the sounds of the storm raging outside his little shelter, Fritz curled up, squeezed his eyes shut, and prayed until he fell asleep.
