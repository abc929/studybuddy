“Where would you like to go today?” asked Peter as he and Father carried Fritz out of the house. Elsie followed with a basket. “Let’s see,” said Fritz, “how about France? I’d love to visit the Eiffel Tower.” “Very funny!” said Peter. “Do you want us to bring you down by the river again?” “How about the top of the hill?” suggested Fritz. “Sounds great!” said Peter. “Fifteen years old today!” said Elsie as she tagged along. “Happy birthday, Fritz!” “Happy birthday to you, too, Elsie!” “Fifteen!” said Father. “I can’t believe it. It seems like just yesterday that you two were toddling around like Flora.” As Fritz was carried up the hill, he felt again, for the thousandth time, how hard it was to have to be carried everywhere. He wasn’t strong enough to try walking yet. He and Elsie had found a Bible passage that had helped him with this, so he had memorized it, and he recited it silently in his mind: God resisteth the proud, and giveth grace to the humble. Humble yourselves therefore under the mighty hand of God, that he may exalt you in due time. After his family had left him on the hilltop, Fritz leaned against a large oak tree. He gazed up into its lofty branches, then closed his eyes and listened. With every whisper of the breeze, the leaves rustled like the majestic rushing of water. He breathed in deeply: earth. How could one ever describe its smell? he thought. He felt the balmy air moving across his skin and the softness of the grass beneath his fingers. He did several minutes of exercises for his arm, stomach, back, and hip muscles. He wanted to be strong, and it felt so good to be able to sit up and scoot around now. Herr Engel’s house was nestled on the other side of the hill. Fritz repositioned himself and gazed upon the lovely, tidy farm. Fritz’s mother was there, selling Herr Engel butter and milk, as she did once a week to him and one other neighbor. Herr Engel had some things that the Vogels didn’t have, like pigs and a quaint little mill sitting along the river to grind his own grain. However, the elderly man didn’t have a cow, and he was grateful to exchange a few small coins each week for a block of butter and a can of milk. Flora hung onto her mother’s skirts. She had turned three last week and followed Mother everywhere. Fritz turned his attention to the immense valley below him. The sweeping hills were dotted with wildflowers. A river curved through the valley like a delicate blue ribbon. In the distance, above the dark forests, billowing white clouds were being chased by darker clouds. Fritz reached into the basket Elsie had packed. Inside was his lunch: a jug of water, black bread wrapped in cloth, and an apple. The Bible was also there, as usual. Today, however, Fritz found a surprise. His fountain pen and a bottle of ink lay beside the story he was writing for Roman, along with a stack of blank paper. Fritz smiled. Elsie thinks it’s time I finished this story. I agree. I’m so close to finishing it. The sun was starting its slow descent in the sky when he finally wrote the words The End. He gave a satisfied sigh. The story had become so exciting, and the characters had taken on such a life of their own that Fritz had to remind himself that the two girls and the door in the tree really were real. Desperately, he wanted to find the tree again and wait there until the girls came, and then find out the real story. But, no. He couldn’t walk. He would never be able to explore the forest again. Unless . . . Fritz sat up straight, an idea forming in his mind. His eyes beamed with excitement as Herr Engel appeared above the crest of the hill, took off his hat, and sat down by Fritz. “Why such bright eyes, Fritz? What is going on in your mind?” Fritz hesitated. The idea was so new and so big. “A horse!” he blurted out. “I want a horse. Oh, Herr Engel, I really want a horse! When I found Roman and rode his horse, I felt so free. I’ll never run again. I’ll never even walk very well. But on a horse—oh, I could move so fast! I could be free. I could go places. I could explore. I—” He couldn’t say more. He leaned back against the tree, his chest heaving. As fast as the idea had buoyed him up, another thought totally deflated him. He closed his eyes. “But it’s impossible. We have no money for a horse.” Herr Engel smiled. “Keep that dream alive, Fritz. I think you’ll find a way to get a horse.” “No, there is no way to do it,” said Fritz. He felt bad for the hollow way the words sounded, but he couldn’t keep his feelings in. “We need pigs before we need a horse, and we can’t even afford those. It’s not fair. So many farms have more than we do. Roman’s family—they have so much. I don’t understand why my family has so little.” Herr Engel sat quietly for a moment, and then very kindly said, “I don’t understand, Fritz, how your family has so much.” “So much? What do you mean?” “I’ll tell you what I mean,” said Herr Engel, gesturing down the hill. “There, over in the field, is a brother, loyal and true. He even lost part of his finger for you.” “What! What do you mean?” “You didn’t know? When he went into that raging blizzard to get the doctor for you, he got frostbite. After the doctor attended to you and Elsie, the doctor amputated half of one of his fingers.” “I didn’t notice. He never said anything,” said Fritz quietly, instantly realizing that, of course, Peter would never say anything. Peter, noble Peter, wouldn’t bear any complaint or grudge. “And over there are bright-eyed little girls that adore you and make life rich with laughter,” continued Herr Engel. “And there in the garden is a twin sister whose undying loyalty and love for her twin brother is unmatched in this world. In your home are two parents, steady and kind. And in the book beside you are the words of Christ that fill your soul, and beside you and above you are the majestic trees and sky and—I could go on,” he ended. “Yes, you could,” said Fritz in almost a whisper. It was quiet on the hilltop except for the deep rushing of the leaves. The wind had picked up, and the sky was now overcast. Fritz put his pen and papers back in the basket. “I finished my story, Herr Engel.” “Oh! Wonderful! Do I get to hear it?” “Of course. I want to read it to my family. We’ll invite you over when I do.” “I can’t wait.” Herr Engel stood up. “You know, you should write your story, Fritz.” “My story?” “Yes. Your life story—all the little things and big things that have happened to you, and what is happening now.” “Why?” Herr Engel put on his hat. “I think it would be good for you, and one day your children might enjoy reading it.” “I don’t know if it would be interesting enough,” Fritz retorted. “Oh, I think every single person on this earth has a story interesting enough to write about.” Herr Engel retrieved an item from his pocket and handed it to Fritz. “Happy birthday, Fritz,” he said, then started off down the hill. Fritz looked at the small, smooth wooden circle in his hand. Tiny words had been carefully carved into it: “Be of Good Cheer.” Fritz closed his hand around the wooden circle and smiled before putting it into his pocket. The more Fritz thought about it, the more he liked the idea of writing his own story. His mind became flooded with exciting and meaningful things he could write about. Words and sentences were already clamoring to be written down, so he took out a fresh piece of paper and scratched a large title across the page: The True Story of Fritz Vogel An hour later, a lone raindrop splattered on the paper. Quickly, Fritz put his Bible and the paper and pen into the basket, scooted the basket toward the tree trunk, and covered it with the blanket he had been sitting on. The sound of raindrops on thousands of leaves filled Fritz’s ears. Sliding and bouncing through the leaves, some of the rain found its way to Fritz. The sun was nowhere to be seen, hidden behind a gray sheet of clouds. A few months ago, he would have been able to dash down the hill and race into the house in less than a minute. Now he had to sit here and wait to be moved. Where is everyone? Don’t they realize I’m getting rained on? Cold, hungry, and feeling forgotten, Fritz started to slip into a dark mood, but he caught himself. He felt for the wooden circle in his pocket. Be of good cheer, he thought. That will always be my motto. As rain dripped from his hair onto his face, he listed the things he was grateful for, all the way from the smell of the rain to a safe home to the dimple in Flora’s cheek. When his father and Peter soon came up the hill to get him, Fritz’s mood was such that he could smile and say, “I’m so glad I don’t need a bath tonight!” When he was in the parlor, drying out by the stove, he found out why he’d been temporarily forgotten. The reason was curled up in front of the stove—a little white dog with a silver bell around its neck. Peter had found it, dirty and bedraggled, down by the river. They didn’t know any neighbors with a dog like that. “That’s the dog in my story!” cried Fritz. “I’m sure of it! What could’ve happened to bring him here, and where are the girls who own him?” That was not the only surprise Fritz received that night. His mother had made Fritz’s favorite dessert—plum cake. And Father could not contain his smile when he presented Fritz with the crutches he had made. “You can try them out tomorrow,” he said. Then Peter spoke up. “Father, I would like your permission to do something for Fritz.” “What is it, son?” Peter looked at Fritz. “I want to take him to school. I am done with school now that I’m sixteen, but Fritz still has a year left, and I want him to finish his schooling more than anything.” “So do I,” said Father. “But, Peter, even if we had a wagon, we couldn’t take him. Because you can walk directly through the forest, you can get to school in just under an hour. But the roads from here don’t lead to the school. How would you take him there?” “On my back.” “On your back!” cried Father. “I’m strong.” “You’re not that strong, Peter,” said Father with a kindly smile. “Please, Father, let me try. I’m quite a bit taller and bigger than Fritz.” “What about the farm work?” “I have a plan. We’ll leave early—right at sunrise. I’ll leave Fritz on the school steps. He won’t mind reading until school starts. I’ll run home, and it will only take me thirty minutes to return, Father, and I’ll be here by the time I usually start working. Then I can run to get him when school is over, and I’ll work after dinner to make up the lost time.” Father sat back in his chair. “Even if you could carry him that far, you will wear yourself out completely with that plan. You might even hurt your back. I don’t approve of it, Peter, I’m sorry.” Peter pushed back his chair and stood up. “Father, I mean to do it. I never ask for anything, but I am asking you to let me do this. I’m begging you to let me do this. School starts up again in a week, and I’m planning on Fritz being there.” Father rubbed his chin, bowed his head quietly for several moments, and then looked up and nodded. “OK, you may try, Peter.” Fritz was pretty sure he saw tears in his father’s eyes. A night from months ago flashed into Fritz’s mind. He remembered Roman expressing his wish to have a brother. He smiled. You were right to desire a brother, Roman.
