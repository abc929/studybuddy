Herr Engel looked up and tilted his paper a little bit away from Fritz, “Oh . . . just something.” “Just something?” Fritz asked, feeling a little puzzled. Herr Engel was usually so open about everything. Herr Engel put down his pen and paper. “Fritz, I’m sure your idea of a grand time during harvest break is taking care of an old man who can’t walk because he cut his foot,” he said with a frown as he lay in the bed that had been made up close to Fritz’s bed. “Only if that man is Herr Engel,” replied Fritz. “Really, it’s my pleasure. It’ll give me a chance to write. Once my family returns from visiting my Aunt Rosa, I’ll have to watch Flora while they’re busy with the harvest. And then winter sets in, and there’s not enough light to write by when I’ll have the time for it.” Fritz saw Herr Engel move position and then grimace a little in pain. “How does your foot feel, Herr Engel? The doctor said the cut is really deep.” “Oh, it comes and goes. Sometimes the pain is really sharp, but most of the time it’s bearable.” Just then, Elsie walked in. “Fritz, I’ve been thinking,” mused Elsie as she put on her hat. “You have to stand up for yourself sometimes; it’s not wrong to do so if you do it in a Christlike way. You should tell our teacher what happened to your poems last month and what Günter did.” “I agree, Elsie. Sometimes it’s wrong not to stand up for yourself, but I feel God whispering to my heart not to stand up to Günter, at least for now.” “Well, I trust that,” replied Elsie. “Maybe the one poem you wrote that morning will still win.” “I really don’t think so. It was unlike any other poem I wrote, and I wrote it quickly. I think the poem was just something I needed to write for myself—to help me through my feelings. I do wish it wasn’t going to take weeks to announce the winner, though.” “Agreed! But I don’t blame our teacher for having a colleague judge the entries. Then it is totally impartial. By the way, did you notice last week how quiet Günter was?” “I did notice that. I also noticed that he doesn’t seem to have a single friend at school. He wanders into the forest during lunch break.” “Maybe he doesn’t want others to see him alone?” “That is what I was thinking.” Fritz sighed, ready to think about something other than Günter. “Well, I’m grateful it’s harvest break. I have a lot to catch up on with my story.” “The True Story of Fritz Vogel,” Elsie stated. “Will you please let me read it sometime, Fritz?” “Actually, yes, you can read it. I’m pretty excited about it. Herr Engel once told me that everyone has a life story interesting enough to write about. He was right.” “Well, maybe you should share your story with the world, Fritz.” Fritz laughed. “I don’t know that it is that interesting, Elsie.” “I think it is!” claimed Elsie. The hired carriage had just pulled up, and Fritz and Elsie joined the rest of the family outside the house. “Thank you for staying with Herr Engel, Fritz. I know Aunt Rosa will miss you,” Maria said. “And I will miss seeing her,” replied Fritz. “But it will be a joy for me to take care of Herr Engel. I’m just grateful he agreed to stay at our house while he recovers.” “Now there is plenty of food to hold you over for four days until we return,” said Maria. “I have several loaves of freshly baked bread. There is cold ham and cheese, and—” “I know, Mother,” said Fritz with a smile. “We definitely will not starve.” “OK, OK. Well, we love you, Fritz,” Maria replied. After the carriage left, Fritz joined Herr Engel in the parlor. “Looks like it’s just us! I guess I’ll join you and start writing myself.” Fritz could get around the house pretty well with his crutches now. He made his way to his little chest in the hallway by the parlor. The stairs, still too hard for Fritz to navigate with his crutches, had made Maria decide that Fritz’s bed would remain in the parlor permanently, but there wasn’t enough room for the chest, so it resided in the hallway. Fritz opened the chest. There was his pen and some blank paper, but his story . . . was gone. Fritz stood up and scratched his head. Gone? Where could it be? At first he was confused, but then he grew alarmed. “Herr Engel,” Fritz said as he moved back into the parlor. “My story is gone. I am positive it was in my chest. What could have happened to it?” Herr Engel rubbed his chin. “Well, you did just tell Elsie she could read it. Maybe she took it with her on her trip.” “But she didn’t ask,” said Fritz with a frown. “You’re right, though, maybe she did take it. Roman took my story about the door in the tree, Elsie—maybe—took The True Story of Fritz Vogel, and Günter gave my poems to the forest.” Fritz sighed. “I was going to work on writing while my family is away.” “Well, do you remember where you left off in the story of your life? Why don’t you just pick up there?” “You’re right! I can do that.” Herr Engel picked up his pen and paper again. “Elsie says if you win the poetry competition that you’ll buy a can of oil big enough to last you all year.” “Did she? Well, it’s true.” Herr Engel smiled, and then he turned his attention to his paper and started writing. “Are you writing a letter to extended family?” Fritz asked. “No,” said Herr Engel, not offering any more information. Fritz was curious, but he didn’t feel it was respectful to push any further, so he began writing too. By lunchtime Fritz noticed that the sky had grown quite dark and ominous. “I think we are in for a storm,” Fritz said to Herr Engel. It wasn’t long before a fierce wind began to blow, rattling the windows and howling through a gap under the door. A cracking sound outside sent Fritz to the window. “One of the trees by the river has been blown down!” Bright light suddenly filled the room, and then two seconds later came the loud rumble of thunder. “Here comes the rain,” said Herr Engel just as the rain began to pour from the clouds. The gushing, rushing sound of thousands of raindrops soon turned into a boisterous pounding. “No!” Fritz pressed his face against the windowpane and watched hailstones bouncing wildly on the ground. “Not hail!” He moved to the front door of the home and opened it, trying to see the fields that Peter and Jakob had cared for so diligently all summer. All he could see, however, were the sheets of hail, falling almost vertically in the wild wind. He watched the hail bounce wildly and cover the land. I’ve never seen hail this large. I can’t believe this is happening. It was all over in ten minutes. The storm had moved down into the valley and left only a light drizzle behind. Fritz moved dejectedly with his crutches across the wet ground, surveying the damaged crops. Broken stalks littered the fields. Everything was ruined except for the potatoes that lay safe underground. Fritz, turning back to the farmhouse, let out a sudden gasp—the roof. Two holes as large as a wagon gaped at him from the long, slanted roof. The wooden shingles had been ripped off by the wind. The roof was old and needed new shingles, but Jakob had not found the time, nor could he justify money to replace the roof yet. That night Fritz struggled to fall asleep. He tossed and turned and then sat up in bed with a sinking heart when he heard rain begin to fall again, knowing that, because of the holes in the roof, the hay in the loft was getting wet and ruined, and neither he nor Herr Engel were fit to do anything to save it. By the early morning hours, the rain had stopped, and Fritz finally fell asleep. However, Fritz was not asleep long when he was suddenly awoken. Something had made a crashing noise, and he thought he had heard voices. The overcast night was unusually dark as Fritz tried to orient himself, his alarm growing. There was a scratching sound in the room above him and the creaking of floorboards. “Father?” Fritz called loudly, wondering if his family had returned in the middle of the night, but knowing that was almost impossible. The sounds stopped. All was still. Then he heard a window opening and muffled voices. Fritz hurried out of bed, grabbed his crutches, and moved to the window. In the shadowy night, he saw two adult figures running down the hill—one pushing something, a cart maybe, and the other appearing to have a bulky bag thrown over his shoulder. Robbers! If only I could move faster! By the time Fritz reached the front door and opened it, he could see no trace of the men. There is nothing to be done, Fritz thought. Nothing. Our crops have been ruined, our roof has been damaged, and we have been robbed all in one day. Fritz lit a lantern and made his way to the kitchen. He reached into the little cupboard where Mother kept her pouch of butter money. It’s here! But suddenly Fritz realized that there was only a small handful of coins in the pouch, yet it had been nearly full before. When Fritz returned to his bed, he heard a soft moan come from Herr Engel’s bed. He gently shook his friend’s shoulder to wake him. “Herr Engel, we’ve been robbed. They took Mother’s butter money, and I don’t know what else. It’s so strange, though, they didn’t take the whole pouch, and they left a small handful of coins. Mother has saved that money for years. Now, how will she ever get her stove? I just can’t bear it.” Herr Engel hesitated but then spoke in a weak voice. “Fritz, don’t worry. They didn’t steal your mother’s money.” “How do you know?” “Because that handful of money is all she has now. Your mother bought your horse with her butter money.” “Bought my horse?” Fritz’s mind was reeling. “Mother bought my horse? It was Mother?” “Yes, Fritz. I wasn’t supposed to tell you, but I think it’s best you know. She didn’t want you to feel bad that she wasn’t going to be able to buy her stove.” Herr Engel then moaned again. “Are you OK?” Fritz asked, his throat tight, thinking of his mother’s sacrifice and love. “Fritz, my foot. It feels like it’s on fire.” After lighting a lamp, Fritz unbandaged Herr Engel’s foot. He was quiet. “What is it?” Herr Engel asked weakly. “How does it look?” “It’s swollen, and . . . Herr Engel, it looks like it’s infected, and . . . your leg is starting to swell too.” Fritz put his hand to Herr Engel’s forehead. It was very hot. “You have a fever.” Fritz gently washed Herr Engel’s wound and bandaged it with clean strips of cloth.
