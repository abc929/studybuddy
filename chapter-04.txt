“What a story!” exclaimed Roman, lying in his four-poster bed with Fritz sitting on a chair by the bedside. Late morning light streamed into the room. The two boys had become fast friends in just the twenty-four hours that Fritz had been in the Winkler home. “I really can’t believe that’s how you finally found Karl Hofer,” said Roman. “Didn’t you ask him anything about painting or being famous?” “No, I was too hungry and worried to think of anything else, but now I wish I had.” “Well,” said Roman. “Maybe you’ll cross paths with him again when you become a famous artist.” “If that ever happens,” frowned Fritz. “My family doesn’t even have enough money to buy a pig for our little farm, let alone send me to be trained as an artist.” “You’ll find a way, Fritz.” “You know,” said Fritz, feeling like he could be very open with Roman, “the day before I got lost in the fog, I stumbled into a man who owns a neighboring farm. He called me . . . the clockmaker’s son.” “What’s wrong with that?” asked Roman. He raised his eyebrows. “Your father is a clockmaker, right?” “Yes, and he’s a very good clockmaker, but it bothered me that the man said that. I just . . . I want to do something big in this world. I want to be known as more than a clockmaker’s son. I feel that I was created for something important. I can’t explain it. It just wells up in me. I don’t want to be average. I don’t want to live and die having been just a clockmaker. I want to do something great.” “I never really think much about my future,” admitted Roman. “I mean, my father owns a successful brush factory that I’ll inherit. People need to brush their hair, right? And I’ll inherit this beautiful house. That’s my future, and I don’t really need more than that. I think your ambition is amazing. I think you will be a famous painter, Fritz.” Roman smiled. “Hey, you should start by painting that tree in the forest—the one with the door in it. It sounds so enchanting the way you described it, all alone in a clearing with big, twisting branches and moss-covered boulders surrounding it.” Fritz smiled. “It was an incredible tree. And the girl with bright red hair and the little white dog— where did they come from? There weren’t any homes for miles around.” “And what were they doing? It’s all so mysterious!” Roman added. “I’m not sure, but last night as I lay in bed, my imagination ran off, and a whole story about them formed in my mind. It’s like each of those girls came alive—their possible personalities, their possible story.” “Oh, tell it to me, Fritz! I’m so bored lying here.” “I can’t right now. Your mother said I could only stay for thirty minutes, and then you had to rest. I think it will be a pretty long story—I’ve thought of so many details. Then the doctor is coming, and then your family is having lunch with you here in your room. Your uncle is coming to visit later, and you’ll have to rest again.” Roman shifted his position and then closed his eyes, feeling suddenly tired as a wave of pain shot through his shoulder. “Too bad. I would love to hear that story.” “I’ll let you rest now,” said Fritz, standing up. “Wait. Over there on my desk,” said Roman, his eyes still closed. “Top drawer. There is a stack of paper and a fountain pen. Take it. I don’t want you to forget the story. Write it down for me, will you?” “Sure!” said Fritz, retrieving the paper and pen. “I’ll read it to you when I’m done.” Fritz began his story right away, sitting at the small desk in the guest room where he was staying. He loved the smell of the paper and the sound of the scratching pen as he wrote a title across the top of the first page: “The Door in the Tree.” Fritz was just at an exciting part of the story when it was time for lunch, but he didn’t mind the break because he experienced things he had never tasted, including sweet tea flavored with cinnamon and large, honey-flavored buns. Back at his desk directly after lunch, Fritz continued scratching away, writing in small letters on the white paper. Time escaped him. He included more details than he had thought of originally, as his imagination continued to build the story and add in some new twists and turns. Oh, if only I really could meet those girls and know their story, thought Fritz. A knock at his bedroom door came later that evening as the room was growing dark and Fritz was squinting to see the words he was writing. Fritz jumped a little, splattering ink on his paper. He got up and opened the door. Frau Winkler stood in the hallway, a smile on her face and a bright lantern in her hand. “Roman told me about the story you are writing. He is quite excited to read it. I thought you could use this,” she said, handing Fritz the lantern. “And I picked these for you in the garden,” said Roman’s little sister, appearing from behind her mother’s skirt with a bowl of strawberries in her hands. “And—” said the mother, handing Fritz a little box, “we just got these chocolates from Switzerland. This box is for you.” Two hours later, Fritz leisurely popped a strawberry and a chocolate in his mouth and kept writing by the glowing light of the lantern. How pleasant this is! he thought. My own room; a feather pillow; smooth, white sheets. No gardening tomorrow. At midnight Fritz quietly closed his window. A gentle breeze had picked up, and it was getting a little too cool. He sat back down at his desk and picked up his pen. It seems the more I write, the further I get from the ending! I need to finish this for Roman. Fritz didn’t finish his story that night, even though he stayed up until 2:00 a.m. Nor did he finish his story the next day, even though he found several hours to write after an exciting tour of the Winklers’ brush factory. Herr Winkler was obviously a respected man. He employed over thirty-five men. “When will you finish the story?” Roman asked Fritz one evening. “You leave tomorrow.” “I’m writing as fast as I can, but the story keeps growing. I hope I can finish it tonight . . . but look!” Fritz pointed to the stack of books on Roman’s nightstand. “It looks like you have plenty of stories to keep you occupied.” “Mother brought those in for me. I haven’t read a single one. I’m not that big on reading.” “May I?” asked Fritz. He stood and touched the top of the stack of books. “Of course,” answered Roman. Fritz opened a few of the books and turned some of the pages, almost reverently. “I saw your library downstairs. Really, I can’t believe that your family has all of these books. Imagine! Three long shelves full of books. We have one book at home, the Bible. Sometimes my teacher at school gets new books, and I devour them during recess. And not only do you have all these books, but you have paper and pens and plum tarts and chocolate and silver spoons! We live in the middle of nowhere, practically isolated from the world, especially in the winter. You have several close neighbors and live just a ten-minute walk from town. You even have your own carriage!” Roman lay back on his pillow and considered Fritz meditatively. “Tell me, Fritz. What is it like at your farm?” Fritz sat down again. “Our farmhouse has been in the family for generations. It’s sturdy but worn, and it’s small. I have one brother and five sisters. Things are pretty crowded at the farm.” “You have a brother?” interrupted Roman. “Now, I’m imagining that! I’ve always wanted a brother. What is it like?” “I don’t know. Peter is quiet, and I’m . . . not. I don’t think we understand each other at all.” “But five sisters,” cried Roman. “How jolly your days must be. Isn’t it fun?” “Fun? Well, I don’t know if I’d call it fun, but Elsie, my twin, is just the best. I don’t think she’s ever said a cross word to me. She understands me and thinks so highly of me, even when I’ve done wrong. She is always cheerful, like my father. Gretchen is only seven, but she can really hold a grudge. She’s mad at me because I ran off to find Karl Hofer. The younger twins . . . they’re always playing with their dolls. We don’t really interact much. Flora is just two, and she’s cute, but she’s fussy an awful lot. It feels so quiet and calm here compared to our cramped and clamorous home.” “I love the big words you use, Fritz,” Roman said with a laugh. “You probably get them from all of those books you read. Don’t you miss your family? You’ve never been away from them for this long, have you?” “No, I haven’t been away from them for even one night, actually. But the funny thing is—I don’t know—I almost feel like I could stay here for months just fine. I mean, sure, I miss them, but I haven’t thought about them much. Staying here has been just incredible.” “What’s your mother like?” Fritz considered the question for a moment before answering. “Mother? She’s quiet. She works hard. She seems to make Father very happy.” “What else is she like?” Fritz felt suddenly very uncomfortable. “I . . . I don’t know. She works. She cooks. She . . . makes wonderful potato soup. Father says so every night.” “You eat potato soup every night?” “Just about, yes.” Fritz felt the color rise in his face. How plain his family seemed. How dull and unrefined his mother suddenly appeared to Fritz. He tried to think of what else she did well. “Her black bread is good too. I’ve never had the kind of white, fluffy rolls you have here before. They’re amazing.” “We get them from the bakery. Mother never bakes. Cakes, rolls, bread, tarts—we buy it all from town. We also eat at the inns often. The food is fantastic, but I want to hear more about the farm.” “Well, Roman, I just don’t think you’d like it. It’s hard work, and there is hardly any time for leisure like I’ve experienced here.” Fritz felt unhappy and said the statement a little snappily. “There’s cleaning out the cow stall, plowing the fields, milking the cow, pulling weeds. That’s all. Now you can see why I want to leave it someday. I don’t want to talk about the farm anymore.” The room was quiet. Roman studied Fritz’s face as Fritz looked sadly out the window. “I want to try it, Fritz.” “Try it? What do you mean?” “I want to try living on a farm. Let’s trade places for a summer. It gets so dull around here. I would love to work with Peter and have a jolly time with so many sisters. I’d love to breathe the mountain air and work with my hands every day—for a summer.” Later that night Herr Winkler heartily agreed to the plan, feeling a summer on a mountain farm would be a great experience for his fourteen-year￾old son—as long as his broken shoulder healed well enough for hard work. Fritz lay in bed that night with a meditative heart. Fall was almost over. The isolated winter would tick by slowly, as they were literally trapped on the farm. He would start learning how to make clocks this winter, which he was dreading. And then . . . it would be spring. He could finish out school during the spring and early summer, and then with late summer break, he could be here again—for the entire summer! A thrilling feeling arose in him, but as he stared out his window at the glittering stars, a thought made his heart sink. Would Father go for the plan?
