Fritz was in the front yard enjoying the atypical warm fall day. Flora sat on his lap, and Gretchen leaned against him. “I just can’t wait for you to meet them today,” said Fritz. Gretchen sat up. “Are they really the girls in your story?” “Yes, they really are the girls, but the story I wrote isn’t the real story that happened to them. It’s quite different, actually,” said Fritz with a smile. Just then, a carriage appeared around the corner. “What?” said Fritz in surprise. “The Köhlers don’t have a carriage. They have a little wagon, and it’s not time for the Köhlers to arrive.” However, not far behind the carriage was a wagon, but it was not the Köhlers’ wagon. Fritz recognized it as Farmer Becker’s wagon. A head popped out of the carriage window. It was Roman, and he was waving! “Roman!” exclaimed Fritz. “What a surprise!” The rest of Fritz’s family had heard the pounding hooves of the big horses pulling the carriage, and they came out to the front yard. Roman jumped out of the carriage and ran to Fritz, and the rest of the Winkler family followed. As the Vogels and Winklers were in the midst of cheerfully greeting each other, Farmer Becker’s wagon pulled up, and the whole Becker family, including Günter, piled out of the wagon. Another wagon came around the bend, and Fritz could see that it was again too big to be the Köhlers’ wagon. Then Herr Becker’s booming voice captured Fritz’s attention. “We hope we are not interrupting a meeting with your friends, Fritz, but Günter has some important matters to take care of, and then I believe a bunch of the neighbors are coming here for a meeting.” “A meeting?” asked Fritz. “What do you mean?” “Let’s let Günter take care of his business first, if you don’t mind,” replied Herr Becker. Günter stepped forward and cleared his throat, looking terribly embarrassed. “Günter, we can go somewhere private if you would prefer,” Fritz offered. “No, it’s OK,” said Günter. “This is something you already know, Fritz, and that everyone else needs to know. I stole your poem—the one that won the competition.” Günter handed Fritz a small bag. “Here are the twenty marks. I know what I did was wrong. I am really sorry, Fritz.” “Günter, thank you. It’s water under the bridge,” Fritz declared. Günter turned to Elsie and Gretchen. “I’m sorry to you as well. I don’t know why I was so mean.” Elsie walked over to Günter and extended her hand. “Let’s shake hands and start over. We’d like to be your friends.” Günter’s face turned red. He shook Elsie’s hand with no words. “There is something else important I need to tell you, Fritz,” began Günter, turning back to Fritz, but loud voices interrupted Günter. A family from a neighboring farm was getting out of the wagon that had just pulled up. Also, the pounding of more hooves filled the air as another carriage approached the farm, followed by yet another large wagon. What in the world is going on? thought Fritz. For several minutes, everything was chaotic. Dust flew from approaching wagons, everyone was greeting each other, Elsie and Roman were talking, and the Winkler parents were talking to Jakob and Maria. Little kids were running around playing tag. Fritz saw his teacher ride up on his horse. Fritz’s mind was whirling. Why is Roman here? Why are my neighbors here? Why is my teacher here, and who is with him? As if things could not get more crazy, two more carriages arrived. A man with a large mustache and a fancy suit exited one carriage. Elsie ran up to the man and started talking excitedly to him, as if she had been expecting him. Fritz saw a thin man in his twenties pull a pad of paper and a pencil out of his bag, look around, and start scribbling away. Jakob called out loudly. “Hello! It looks like everyone is here.” The crowd quieted down, and the mothers gathered their little children around them and hushed the children’s excited chatter. Jakob spoke again. “Well, I know why you are all here, and you all know why you are all here, but Fritz does not know why you are all here. Should we tell him?” The crowd cheered, and Fritz looked at his father quizzically. Jakob smiled at his son and winked at him. Then he turned back to the crowd. “Herr Becker, why don’t you explain the reason for this gathering.” Herr Becker took off his hat and paused. It was so quiet that Fritz could hear the river gurgling below the farm. “Fritz,” began Herr Becker, “it seems that you have a particular liking for the Bible.” That was not what Fritz was expecting, and he just looked at Herr Becker with a confused look. Herr Becker continued, “It seems that you’ve got most of the valley reading the Bible, and your sermon at Herr Engel’s funeral brought tears to all of our eyes. So, you see, we decided that we need a church built in this area, and we have found out that one will be built here within a few years. We all want you, Fritz Vogel, to be the minister of the church as soon as you can.” “Minister?” Fritz was stunned. “We have made arrangements for your training—that is, if you would like to be a minister.” The crowd stood hushed and still as a mountain, all eyes on Fritz. Fritz knew instantly that there was nothing in the world he would rather do than be a minister—to share God’s word, comfort the weary, bring people closer to Christ. A huge smile spread across his face. “Yes! Yes, I would love that.” The crowd cheered, and the man in the fancy suit came forward to Fritz, and Elsie clapped her hands. The skinny man with the notepad came forward too. “Fritz Vogel,” said the man, extending his hand, “it’s a pleasure to meet you. I work with Schneider and Fischer publishing. Your sister copied the book you are writing, The True Story of Fritz Vogel. She sent me the copy, and, well, I came to tell you that we would like to publish the book. Fascinating story! Absolutely fascinating! I also heard about this event today, which will make a fine climax to your book. I wanted to witness it myself.” Before Fritz could say anything, Roman called out. “Unbelievable! Fritz, that is why my family is here. I gave your book The Door in the Tree to another publisher, and yesterday we received a letter from the publisher. They want to talk to you about publishing the book!” The crowd cheered again, and Fritz felt overwhelmed. He choked back tears. A scene flashed into his mind—lying in bed with his injuries, feeling that his whole life was over. He would have never imagined all of this happening. He knew that none of it would have happened without Herr Engel introducing Fritz to the light and hope found through Jesus Christ. “What do you say about all this, Fritz?” cried his teacher. Fritz looked out at the crowd, and he could only say the words from Psalm 107: “O give thanks unto the Lord, for He is good.” A short time later, the crowd began to disassemble and leave. Herr Becker’s family hung back by their wagon. When everyone was gone, Günter approached Fritz. He held out Herr Engel’s Bible. “I’d like to give this back to you,” he said. “Oh, you can keep it,” replied Fritz. “I want you to read it.” “I did.” “You did? The whole thing?” Günter looked embarrassed. “Yes. I—I felt so terrible after your stolen poem won that I just wanted to do anything to get rid of the feeling. I flipped open the Bible one night, and once I started reading, I just couldn’t stop. For days I just pored over the words. I thought I was bad and would always be bad and that no one really . . . cared for me. But these words told me differently.” “I know what you mean,” said Fritz. “I asked my mother to send me a Bible,” Günter said, “so you can keep this one.” Fritz took the Bible and laughed. “I bet your mother was surprised to get that request!” Just then Fritz saw the Köhlers’ little wagon coming up the road. “Come on, Günter, I want you to meet some people.” “Oh, wait. There’s one thing first. There is a half sheet of paper that was folded up and put near the back of the Bible. It is from Herr Engel. It’s very important, Fritz. I’m sorry I didn’t get it to you sooner.” Fritz opened the Bible excitedly. “A note from Herr Engel!” said Fritz. “I can’t wait to read it.” “Oh, it’s more than a note,” Günter declared. Fritz scanned the small, neat handwriting on the page, and then looked up in shock. “I can’t believe it. I just can’t believe it!”
