Fritz had a plan. “Father, when you all go to town this morning to sell potatoes and the clocks, may I go on a long ride with Chocolate?” “Sure, Fritz, but are you sure you don’t want to come with us into town?” “I’m sure, Father.” “Where are you going to ride?” “I thought I would ride up to the abbey ruins. I’ve never been there, and it’s only five miles away, which is not too far on horseback.” “The stories about that place don’t worry you?” “Not at all.” “All right, then. Be safe, son. Make sure you are home well before dark. We will be back by late morning tomorrow.” As soon as they disappeared around the corner in their neighbor’s wagon, Fritz filled a flask of water, gathered a bundle of food, and tied a shovel over the basket on Chocolate’s side. “We’re going to save the farm, Chocolate. You and me. Today! We are going to find that money that a nun buried at the abbey long ago,” said Fritz as they galloped away from the farmhouse. Their cow, which had been sick, died yesterday—a final blow to the family that made it seem sure that they would lose the farm. Jakob had started making plans for what they would do next spring. They might have to split up for a little while, with some of the family going with Aunt Rosa, and Elsie and Peter finding work where they could. All the farms in the area had been hit by the hailstorm. Their neighbors had helped as much as they could, but it wouldn’t be enough to save the farm. One problem they couldn’t figure out was who would take care of Herr Engel’s animals. They had been taking care of them until Herr Engel’s extended family could be located to inherit the farm. The heaviness of all the recent losses, mixed with the uncertainty of the future, weighed on the family. Fritz couldn’t bear the thought of Peter not working the land on their farm. He couldn’t bear the thought of losing the little piece of heaven with the river and the beautiful hill and the memories flowing around the farm everywhere. He also couldn’t bear the thought of losing Chocolate, for there was nowhere to keep him at Aunt Rosa’s. “Come on, Chocolate,” Fritz said, giving his horse a little kick with his feet. “We need all the time we can get to look for the treasure.” In less than an hour, the abbey ruins loomed above Fritz. It felt very quiet and lonely. A thick forest of tall pines loomed behind the abbey, framing the crumbling gray stones in green. The breeze whispered with a quiet rush through the top of the trees. Fritz quickly found an area that had been dug recently, most likely by the Winklers. Fritz decided to start close to that area. He tied Chocolate to a tree and let him graze on the abbey grass, and then he went to work. The first clank of the shovel echoed through the abbey and sounded inordinately loud. Fritz hesitated for a moment and looked around. There were no visitors, likely because of the overcast skies, or maybe more likely because of the stories surrounding the place. Fritz knew it would probably rain, but he didn’t mind getting wet. After fifteen minutes, a mist moved in, making the abbey seem even more lonely and mysterious. A light sprinkle began to fall, and Fritz found it refreshing, even though he could feel the slight chill of late autumn in it. It was hard work, since Fritz had to shovel sitting down and didn’t have the leverage of standing up. It didn’t take long until he felt that his task was completely hopeless. The money could be anywhere. The ground was rocky, and after an hour Fritz had not been able to dig much. A sudden lightning flash not far away spooked Chocolate, who was on the far end of the abbey. A few seconds later, a streak of lightning hit a tree just outside the abbey walls. The electric crack that sizzled and snapped in the air seemed to vibrate through Fritz. The sound was deafening. Fritz’s hair stood up on end, and he fell to the ground in fear. Just as he was covering his head with his arms, he saw Chocolate bolting down the hill. Ah! thought Fritz. I didn’t tie the rope tightly enough! Another crack of lightning a couple miles away kept Chocolate running. The fast-moving storm was quickly gone, but the mist and light sprinkling remained, and Fritz could not see anything past the abbey walls. He knew Chocolate would keep running until he was back at the farm. Fritz placed his head in his hands and cried, his shoulders shaking with his sobs. It was all too much. He had borne his trials with as much hope and cheer as he could, but he felt he could bear them no more. There were no homes in sight. No people. Just walking to the bottom of the hill would take Fritz a long time, and he knew he couldn’t walk much farther than that. He shivered, the misty rain no longer feeling refreshing. He looked around at the lonely abbey and felt panic. Oh, Father in Heaven, he cried, please help me. Thou hast said that Thou wouldst not leave those who follow Thee comfortless. Thou art my strength. I believe that Thou canst work wonders. Work Thy wonders now, I pray. I need Thy help. Please help me, Father. He buried his face in his arm again. “Hello?” Fritz jumped a little and looked up. He blinked his eyes. Standing in front of him, with the mist and the old abbey walls behind her, was the mysterious red-headed girl about his age that he had seen in the forest. Her blue eyes sparkled, and wisps of her curly hair had escaped out of her kerchief and blew in the breeze. She carried a basket covered with a cloth. “Do you need help?” the girl asked. Fritz blinked. He could not believe the girl was standing before him. “Yes! I—I lost my horse. He was spooked by the lightning and ran off. I am crippled, and I cannot walk far enough to find help.” “Well, it looks like help found you.” “Yes, it does. But . . . who are you?” “I’m Lisette Köhler. And you are Fritz.” Fritz’s eyes widened. “Don’t be scared,” Lisette laughed. “You saw me at the tree, I know. We could never go there again because of you, but that’s OK. You also saw me at school that one day. I saw you watching me through the window.” Fritz had a million questions tumbling in his mind, but only one came out now. “But how do you know my name?” “Well, when we saw our dog with you and the other boy swimming at the river, we snuck up to you, hoping our dog would see us without you noticing, and I heard the other boy call you Fritz.” Fritz relaxed. “We live close to here,” Lisette said. “Really? I didn’t think there were any houses near here.” “Well, there is one tiny house—kind of. Nobody knows about it because it is tucked into the forest. No one seems to like going into this part of the forest. But we can talk later. I’ll go get my brother, and we’ll get you out of this rain. I’ll be back in about fifteen minutes.” As Fritz waited for Lisette’s return, he marveled over the events that had just unfolded. He even wondered if he was actually dreaming. It all seemed surreal. But Lisette and a real boy came out of the forest with a real cart. “This is my older brother, Garit,” announced Lisette. Garit smiled. He looked about eighteen years old and had red hair and a cheerful smile like Lisette. He was broad shouldered and strong, which Fritz was grateful for as Garit pushed the cart through the rough forest path toward their little home. Their home was, indeed, small. Fritz was not sure if it could really be called a home. “It was once a woodcutter’s hut, I believe,” said Lisette as Fritz made his way slowly inside. The little white dog was there. She barked and then flew to Fritz, jumping up and down. “Oh! It’s great to see you, too!” said Fritz, petting the dog. The younger girl he had seen with Lisette before sat by a glowing and crackling fire. “This is Heidi, my little sister,” explained Lisette. Heidi smiled and pulled a chair over to the fire. “Sit here and dry out your clothes.” Lisette brought Fritz a cup of drinking chocolate. Fritz gratefully took the warm, elegant cup painted with pale, pink flowers and lined with silver around the rim, but he looked at it quizzically. “What is it?” asked Lisette. “Well, this is a very expensive cup, and, well, . . . drinking chocolate is . . .” “Expensive,” finished Lisette. “I know it must seem strange that we have such a cup and drink chocolate while living in this tiny, run-down hut.” “It’s because we just found the money,” blurted out Heidi. “Just barely a few days ago. Drinking chocolate is Lisette’s favorite, and she missed it so. It is one of the first things we bought, along with this cup because Lisette thought it was so lovely. Garit insisted she buy it.” “You found the money?” asked Fritz. “You mean at the abbey? You found the money that the nun hid in the abbey?” “No,” said Garit. “We heard about that, but that money was found over ten years ago.” “Ten years ago!” Fritz exclaimed. “Really?” “Yes,” replied Garit. “Apparently, it was found by a great-great-grandnephew of the nun. He was really quiet about it.” “How do you know this?” “My late father. It’s the nun’s story, actually, that brought us here.” “I am so confused,” admitted Fritz. “We can start from the very beginning of the story, if you would like,” Lisette offered. “Yes, please!” cried Fritz as he sipped some of the chocolate and felt the warmth of the fire on his back.
