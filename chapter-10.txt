The scratching of a pen echoed lightly in the parlor. Fritz sat at the desk, golden beams of morning sunlight dancing through his dark hair as he wrote his story. The tops of the trees were whipped and whirled about as if by a giant who had come smashing through the forest. The storm was terrible. I curled up tightly on my bed of prickly pine needles, hoping there weren’t any other living things sharing the shelter of the pine with me. A noise pulled Fritz’s mind from his story. His mother was opening the little hatch between the kitchen and the parlor. She slid a plate of steaming pancakes through the opening. “Pancakes?” asked Fritz. “What’s the special occasion?” “The special occasion is you trying out your crutches, of course!” she answered back through the hatch. “Mother, is there a chance I could talk to you before the others come in for breakfast?” In a few moments, his mother appeared in the parlor with Flora holding onto her skirts. “What is it, Fritz?” Maria said, as the white dog also bounded in and snuggled up to Fritz’s legs. Fritz petted the dog. “You work so hard, Mother. I’ve been watching you. I want to help you.” “Oh!” Maria was happily surprised. She sat down in a chair, and Flora immediately climbed into her lap. “That’s so kind of you. I’m fine, really, though. I’ve worked hard my whole life. What were you thinking of helping with?” “I know I can’t do a lot of things without my feet working well. I’ve prayed to know how I can help you. Sitting around a lot has given me a chance to see things I didn’t use to see. You never get a moment for yourself, and Flora is constantly needing you. I feel God has answered my prayer and shown me a way to help you. I’m wondering what you think of ‘Fritz and Flora time’?” Flora looked over at Fritz at the mention of her name. Fritz continued, “I was thinking that each morning after breakfast, I could take Flora for half an hour and tell her stories and sing with her, so you can have some time for yourself.” In a flash Flora jumped off her mother’s lap and hopped up onto Fritz’s lap. Settling against her older brother, she said, “Fritz and Flora time. I’m ready.” Mother laughed. “Fritz, what an idea! Flora would love it. I would love it. But I truly don’t think I could take thirty minutes to myself. Why, I don’t even know what I would do, and there is so much to be done around the home.” “Well, what about fifteen minutes?” countered Fritz. “What if you read the Bible for fifteen minutes? Reading the Bible every day has changed my life, Mother. I want you to feel the same light and joy by reading every day.” Maria pursed her lips and thought. Then she nodded, her dark eyes sparkling. “OK, I will! Let’s try it. Thank you, Fritz.” An hour later Fritz, equipped with his new crutches, leaned against the home next to his father. The smooth blue sky, the sunshine lining the edges of the clouds, and the melody of birds made everything seem hopeful and happy. “Father, thanks for understanding why I didn’t want anyone else here.” “Of course. I wouldn’t want a crowd watching me either.” He gestured to the dog sitting nearby. “Hopefully you don’t mind the dog watching. He seems to like you.” “Both of us sleeping in the parlor is a bonding experience, I guess.” Jakob laughed. “OK now, what the doctor said is that you will need to learn how to walk in a different way than you used to. Your hip is a little crooked and won’t swing quite how it used to. Your feet are twisted, so they are not pointing straight forward anymore, and some of the bones likely were too crushed to heal, so you’ll need to learn how to walk on them differently.” “OK, Father.” Fritz’s heart was pounding. I’m going to walk again. How good it will feel. He pushed himself away from the side of the house, held the crutches, and stood up straight. Almost instantly, he wobbled wildly, and Jakob caught him in his strong arms. “I’ll steady you, son, and then let go. Then you can try standing again.” For several minutes, Jakob steadied his son, let go, and then caught Fritz again when he started to fall after just a few seconds. Finally, Fritz broke into a sob. “I can’t even stand up! I can’t do this! I’ll never walk! I’ll never walk again.” Jakob took his son in his arms. “Fritz, every day I will be here with you. Every day we will try again. Every day you will get stronger. I promise. The only way you will never walk again is if you give up.” Fritz closed his wet eyes and hung his head, but then nodded in agreement. “Good then. We’ll try again tomorrow.” Just then, the dog started barking. A carriage was seen coming down the road to their home— not a common occurrence. Jakob carried Fritz into the parlor before the carriage arrived. It turned out to be Roman and his family. They all sat in the parlor after introductions were made. Fritz sat at the table, which hid his braces. “I’m sorry we didn’t give you advance notice about our visit,” said Herr Winkler. “We were passing through the area today anyway. Roman and Fritz had the idea of trading places this summer—for the experience—and I, uh, I thought we would stop by and see if that would work out. I thought a summer of mountain air and hard work would be good for Roman. I also know Fritz wants to be a painter. We have an artist living in town, and I can arrange private lessons for him while he is with us.” A moment of heavy silence passed through the room before Jakob responded. “That is an incredible offer, Herr Winkler. I’m overwhelmed. I know we would all be delighted. Fritz, however, had an accident shortly after he returned from your home. He fell out of a tree, and, well, his legs are permanently injured.” Frau Winkler let a heartfelt gasp of concern escape into the silent room, and Fritz’s face reddened as he saw Roman’s eyes widen and his jaw drop. Jakob continued. “Fritz can’t walk—yet. I’m afraid he wouldn’t be able to go right now.” “Of course,” replied Herr Winkler, looking troubled. “We understand, and we are so sorry to hear of this difficult news.” Jakob put his hand to his chin. “We would still love to have Roman stay with us, though. Maybe even just for a week or two. Fritz could use the company, and we would be so happy to have him. What do you think, Herr Winkler?” “Oh, Father,” said Roman, “may I, please?” All the details were settled, and after a joyful lunch, Roman grabbed his bag of things from the carriage and waved goodbye to his family as they drove away. That afternoon, down by the river, Fritz and Roman chattered away like old friends suddenly reacquainted. Roman explained the healing of his shoulder, and Fritz explained the healing from his accident. The exciting news of Fritz finishing his story and finding the white dog was shared. Roman didn’t ask Fritz his feelings about his injuries. For now, he kept the conversation as light as he could. “Where is your family going?” asked Fritz. “To see the ruins of a castle—the one with the large abbey that is still somewhat intact. You should know the place, Fritz. It’s only five miles from here.” “Oh, yeah, I know the place, but I haven’t been there. I’ve heard a lot of stories about that abbey that keep people away from it.” “Exactly!” said Roman excitedly. “You see, we have found out that the stories aren’t true. Well, actually, one story about it is true, my family thinks, and that is why they are going there.” “What do you mean?” “Well, my uncle is a bookseller, and he came across this old journal that was in a crate of books sold to his shop. In it the journal writer describes an incredible story. Supposedly, a nun in the abbey a couple hundred years ago inherited a large amount of money before she became a nun. Not knowing what to do with it, she buried it beneath a walnut tree just outside the abbey. She only told one person right before she died. She sent him a letter. Apparently, that person had died before she even wrote the letter, so he never received it. Instead, the unopened letter got put in a trunk with other letters and books. It wasn’t until generations later that the letter was found by her great-great-grandnephew. He was old, and his last journal entry said that he had spent a week digging at the ruins and had found nothing, so he was giving up. He wrote that he made up stories about the place to keep people away, but there were no more journal entries, so we think he might have died before he could go back.” “Wow!” exclaimed Fritz. “Do you really think there is money buried there?” “I don’t know. Father thought it would be fun to visit the place and just check it out. We’ll have to see what he says when he picks me up.” “What would you do if you found that money yourself, Roman?” “I’m not really sure—travel maybe. What about you?” “I know exactly what I’d do. I’d buy a horse.” “A horse?” “A horse would take me to all the places I can’t go now—school, the neighbors, and even the forest to search for the tree with the door. A horse would allow me to move fast and free.” “Oh, yes, Fritz—a horse would be amazing for you.” “Well, I’m sorry you are missing out on going to the castle with your family, Roman.” “Oh, there’s nowhere I’d rather be right now than here. However, I don’t know how much longer I can wait to hear the story you wrote about the door in the tree.” As if on cue, Jakob and Peter appeared, ready to take Fritz home. “Herr Engel just arrived,” said Peter, “and we’re roasting hazelnuts. We can’t wait to hear the first part of your story, Fritz.”
