Chocolate galloped down the grassy hill to Herr Engel’s house as Fritz breathed in the fresh summer air. He delighted in the feel of the wind ruffling up his hair. He was now able to mount Chocolate by himself, and riding on Chocolate gave him a deep sense of freedom. In fact, the few weeks since he had gotten Chocolate had been some of the happiest weeks he could remember. Herr Engel had just fed the pigs when he saw Fritz approach. “Hello, Fritz! Beautiful day, huh? What brings you here?” Fritz came to a stop. “Well, Herr Engel. I came to thank you . . . for Chocolate. I can’t think of anyone else that could have given him to me.” Herr Engel took off his hat and wiped his forehead with the back of his hand. “It wasn’t from me, Fritz.” “It wasn’t?” “Nope. I promise. I have no idea who gave him to you.” Fritz looked out across the fields. “I don’t understand.” After a few moments, Fritz remembered the other reason he had come. “Oh, Herr Engel, I brought you something.” Fritz reached into his basket and took out four little bundles of flowers, each tied with a bow made of long, braided grass. “Flora and I have some time together each day, and we made these this morning. They’re for your family’s graves. I hope that’s OK.” “OK? Fritz, that makes my heart sing with joy. Thank you!” A little later, Fritz was back in the parlor. Afternoon sunlight streamed through the open window, dancing around him as he poured himself into writing his story. I have been back at school for a week. These have been joyful days, indeed. The smell of books and chalk, new words and ideas—all these things seem even more precious after their absence from my life. I hitch Chocolate right to the front of the school. Several of my classmates’ fathers worked together to create a ramp into the school where there used to be stairs. Someone told me Herr Engel organized and led that project. With my crutches I can now walk up the ramp and to my seat. It’s very slow and awkward. It’s even a little painful to walk, so I can never go far. Günter sneers at me and makes faces, but it doesn’t bother me. I feel triumphant each time I reach my desk, even though I know I could hardly take a step more. Each day one of my sisters rides behind me to school on Chocolate. On the way home, however, Father has given me permission to ride for an hour or two in the forest each day in search of the tree with the door. He says the exercise, sunshine, and fresh air will be good for me. I can cover a lot of ground with Chocolate, and I have a system worked out so that I don’t get lost or search the same area twice. I know I’ll find the tree! While I ride, I think of poems. I keep a pencil and paper in my pocket and stop to jot down lines when they come to me. I’ve written twenty poems already! It’s not hard to write poetry when there’s so much in this forest to inspire me. I have found a thousand subjects. For example, I just wrote a poem about the little mushrooms I saw in the forest yesterday. “Fritz, you got a letter!” cried Elsie as she burst into the parlor. “You all made it back from town!” replied Fritz as he set down his pen. “A letter? It must be from Roman. I wrote to him a couple weeks ago.” Elsie handed him the letter. “Before I read it,” said Fritz, “tell me how the trip to town went. Was it awkward riding into town with Günter?” “Well, with Mother and Father there, Günter didn’t dare do anything mean, but he did manage a couple of sneers. Herr Becker is always so nice to take us to town in his wagon. Mother sold the three embroidered handkerchiefs she made, and Father was able to sell all four crates of extra carrots from our garden. We got supplies, and I got new shoes. But . . . everyone stared at me—at my face. I imagined you standing beside me, Fritz, and it helped me be strong. And then we checked the post office. Father got a couple letters, and you got this one. Come on, open it!” Fritz carefully unsealed the envelope, excited to read the first letter he had ever received. Partway through, he stopped silently reading and looked up at Elsie. “Elsie, Roman wrote a paragraph for you. I hope you don’t mind, but I told Roman that Günter was mean to you about your injuries. Here, read what he wrote to you.” Elsie sat down, took the letter, and read aloud. Elsie, I heard about Günter. Don’t give his words a second thought. When I was at the farm that week, I didn’t even see the scar on your face after the first few minutes I was with you. All I saw was a girl who was wonderful on the inside and the outside. You have a heart of gold, and a light and cheer about you that is as refreshing as the rising sun in the morning. If I were there, I would teach Günter a thing or two for hurting your heart, except I don’t think Fritz would let me, and he is right. But keep your chin up and remember that your scar is not even seen by those who care for you. Fritz looked up and saw that Elsie was blushing and her eyes were misty. “Wonderful on the outside and inside?” she asked. Fritz took the letter. “Yeah, Roman was . . . well, he was direct, but he was right.” Elsie stood up, a huge smile across her face. “I think I’ll go tell Mother.” And with that, she was out of the room and skipping down the hall. Fritz chuckled and read the rest of the letter. I wish I had bought the horse, Fritz, but I honestly know nothing about it. Chocolate! I can’t wait to meet him one day. I am so excited that you have a horse. If you find the tree with the door, please write right away and tell me about it. Also, yes, I am trying to read the Bible every day like you invited me to do. I admit I don’t always remember, but I will keep trying. I do feel something missing on the days I don’t read. Mother and Father send you their best, as do I. Your friend, Roman Fritz folded up the letter, then bowed his head. Dear, Father, he prayed. I thank Thee for all Thy great blessings in my life. I thank Thee for the blessing of a good friend. I thank Thee for my dear family, for the chance to go to school, and for the words that come so easily into my mind. I thank Thee for Thy words that fill my soul. Father, . . . give me strength that I may face the long winter when it comes.
